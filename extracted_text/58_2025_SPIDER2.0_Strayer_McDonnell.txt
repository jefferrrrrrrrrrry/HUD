Abstract
Driving is a complex multisensory experience that requires the integration of
various sensory inputs to maintain effective situational awareness,with vision
and visual attention being paramount for safe driving. However, multitask-
ing significantly degrades a driver’s situational awareness and causes them to
overlook or misjudge important aspects of their environment, such as pedes-
trians, road signs, or other vehicles. It also impairs a driver’s ability to visually
scan for hazards and process vital information, reducing their capacity to
notice and respond to changes on the roadway. Multitasking can also in-
duce inattentional blindness, causing drivers to miss important information
directly in their line of sight. Beyond diminished visual attention, multitask-
ing also slows reaction times to detected events, increasing the likelihood
and severity of crashes. This article discusses the central role that visual at-
tention plays in a driver’s situational awareness, examines common methods
for assessing visual attention while driving, and presents an updated re-
view of the SPIDER (scanning, predicting, identification, decision-making,
and executing a response) model of driver awareness with a focus on visual
distraction.

INTRODUCTION
Driving involves a complex set of learned behaviors that enable safe navigation of a motor vehicle
from one location to another. This task demands good situational awareness, allowing for mo-
torists to recognize actual and potential hazards on the roadway (Strayer & Fisher 2016). Drivers
must execute timely evasive actions, such as steering or braking, should a threat materialize. Ad-
ditionally, maintaining lateral and longitudinal control is essential; drivers should remain within
their lane of travel and keep a safe following distance from other vehicles. Drivers must also adjust
their speed according to driving conditions, which may require traveling below the posted speed
limit. Safe driving involves not only effective situational awareness and timely evasive actions but
also managing distractions, predicting the behaviors of other road users, and adapting to adverse
weather conditions. Additionally, drivers must be prepared for emergencies, understand vehicle
dynamics, and communicate effectively with others on the road while adhering to traffic laws.
Thus, driving is a skill that can take years to master. In fact, motor vehicle crashes are the lead-
ing cause of accidental injury deaths in the United States (NHTSA 2015). Inexperienced drivers
face an even greater risk of being involved in such crashes (NHTSA 2023). The introduction
of graduated driver licensing laws, which impose restrictions on novice drivers—like prohibiting
nonfamily members in the vehicle for the first 6 months—has led to significant reductions in acci-
dents, with fatal crashes decreasing by 38% and injury crashes by 40% (Baker et al. 2007, Hedlund
et al. 2003). Crash rates tend to plateau around the age of 25, coinciding with the full maturation
of the prefrontal cortex (Watson et al. 2011). This suggests that attentional control and decision-
making abilities improve as the brain matures, reducing risky behaviors and poor judgment that
contribute to crashes by younger drivers (Foy et al. 2016).
Driving is a complex multisensory experience that demands the integration of various sensory
inputs to maintain effective situational awareness. Of the five human senses, vision is the most
critical for driving. Drivers rely on visual input to maintain lane position, sustain an appropriate
following distance from the vehicle ahead of them, and respond to changes in driving conditions.
Vision enables the detection of other vehicles, pedestrians, traffic signals, and potential hazards in
the environment, as well as the assessment of speed and depth perception. Fixating on objects in
central vision is crucial for driving tasks such as reading road signs, gauging the speed and distance
of nearby vehicles, and navigating lane markers. Furthermore, drivers typically steer in the direc-
tion they are looking and gaze in the direction they are steering (Cooper et al. 2013, Land & Lee
1994,Navarro et al.2020).In addition to forward vision, motorists use side and rearview mirrors to
maintain situational awareness of surrounding traffic (Large et al. 2016, Mazzae et al. 2023, Mole
& Wilkie 2017). Thus, for effective driving, drivers must continuously scan their environments—
not only to effectively direct the vehicle to where they are headed but also to be ready to respond
to unexpected events.
Foveal vision is not the only aspect of vision that is critical for driving. Peripheral vision plays
an equally important role, as it enables drivers to detect motion and identify objects outside their
central line of sight, enhancing overall situational awareness (Gugerty 2011, Wolfe et al. 2017).
Other characteristics of vision like night vision and color discrimination assist with recognizing
brake lights, turn signals, and road signs in varying environmental lighting conditions ( Jones et al.
2022, Wood 2020). Any impairment in visual acuity, contrast sensitivity, or field of vision can
significantly increase the risk of accidents (Anderson et al. 2021).
While vision is central to safe driving, auditory and haptic senses further enhance a driver’s
ability to process and respond to dynamic conditions (Durso et al. 2007; Endsley 1995, 2015;
Geitner et al. 2019; Horrey et al. 2006; Kass et al. 2007). Auditory cues provide drivers with critical
information that may not be visible, such as the sound of a horn alerting to a potential collision,

MULTITASKING
Multitasking diverts a driver’s attention from the primary task of driving, significantly increasing
the risk of a crash (Gershon et al. 2019, McEvoy et al. 2005, Redelmeier & Tibshirani 1997).1 A
common form of multitasking involves the use of a cell phone to talk, text, or interact with a device
while driving (National Center for Statistics and Analysis 2021). Driving and cell phone use are
functionally independent tasks (Strayer et al. 2022a,b), each with separate goals—transportation
versus communication—and distinct stimuli, such as the driving environment versus the content
of the conversation, as well as different responses (e.g., steering and braking versus talking and
listening). Regan et al. (2011, p. 1772) defined this type of multitasking as “the diversion of atten-
tion away from activities critical for safe driving toward a competing activity, which may result in
insufficient or no attention to activities critical for safe driving.”
In contrast, interdependent tasks work together toward the same overall goal, such as maintain-
ing vehicle control by checking the speedometer, adjusting the accelerator to manage speed, and
steering to stay in the lane. Regan et al. (2011, p. 1775) defined the impairment from performing
interdependent tasks as “insufficient or no attention to activities critical for safe driving brought
about by the driver focusing attention on one aspect of driving to the exclusion of another, which
is more critical for safe driving.” Therefore while interdependent tasks may complement one an-
other to achieve the shared goal of maintaining control of the vehicle, misallocating attention
among them can also compromise safety.
Driving impairments can occur due to competing demands on visual processing, such as when
drivers divert their gaze from the road to check a cell phone display. This diverts crucial visual
attention away from detecting hazards, traffic signals, or other vehicles (Amini et al. 2023). Ad-
ditionally, manual distractions arise when drivers remove their hands from the steering wheel
to perform manual tasks such as texting, eating, or adjusting the radio, reducing their ability to
control the vehicle (Moslemi et al. 2021). Last, cognitive distractions arise when a driver’s focus
shifts away from processing the essential information needed for safe driving, such as when think-
ing about non-driving-related tasks or engaging in conversation, leading to slower reaction times
and decreased situational awareness (D’Addario & Donmez 2019, Salvucci 2019). Together, these
distractions compromise overall driving performance and significantly increase the likelihood of
accidents (NSC 2024).

Visual and manual sources of interference often co-occur and are examples of structural inter-
ference (Strayer et al. 2019; Wickens 1980, 2008). A typical example is using a cell phone to send
or read a text message, where the driver cannot simultaneously focus on both the phone’s display
and the road ahead. In this scenario, both visual (gazing at the phone) and manual (holding the
phone and typing) demands compound,amplifying distraction and increasing the likelihood of im-
paired driving performance. Naturalistic driving studies have shown that glancing away from the
road significantly increases crash risk, with longer glances correlating with greater crash likelihood
(Simons-Morton et al.2014).Atwood et al.(2018) found that drivers who text more frequently face
a higher risk of crashing, with the rate of severe crashes increasing by 0.58% for every additional
text sent per day. Simply put, failing to look at the road leads to crashes or veering off the roadway.
When humans perform two or more attention-demanding tasks concurrently, one task often
prospers at the expense of the other(s) (Kahneman 1973; Navon & Gopher 1979; Norman &
Bobrow 1975; Strayer & Drews 2007a; Wickens 1980, 1984, 2008). For instance, during a cell
phone conversation, a sawtooth pattern of resource allocation emerges, with driving and the
conversation competing for the same limited cognitive resources (Castro et al. 2019, Drews
et al. 2008, Tillman et al. 2017). Notably, drivers experience greater interference from speaking
than from listening during phone conversations (Strayer et al. 2017). Furthermore, the cognitive
distraction caused by cell phone conversations is similar whether using handheld or hands-free
devices (Caird et al. 2018, McEvoy et al. 2005, Redelmeier & Tibshirani 1997, Strayer & Johnston
2001, Strayer et al. 2015).
Multitasking can also create cross talk between tasks, particularly when they involve similar or
overlapping information (e.g., Bergen et al. 2013; Navon & Miller 1987; Pashler 1994a,b). No-
tably,Howard et al.(2020) used evidence accumulation modeling to demonstrate that multitasking
differs from simply increasing task difficulty. While both reduce the rate of evidence accumula-
tion, multitasking additionally raises the response threshold, whereas increased task difficulty does
not. This finding indicates that multitasking imposes an extra cognitive cost, reflecting a strategic
adjustment made to accommodate performing multiple tasks simultaneously (Howard et al. 2020).
MEASURING VISUAL ATTENTION AT THE WHEEL
Given the critical role that vision and visual attention play in maintaining situational awareness
while driving, it is essential to explore how visual attention and the visual distraction arising from
multitasking can be effectively assessed in real time. This is an essential step in identifying the
conditions in which visual attention may deteriorate and in developing reliable driver monitoring
systems that track visual engagement and can alert the driver to reengage attention back to the
roadway (Biondi et al. 2024a).
Visual occlusion is a method used in driving research to simulate moments when visual in-
formation is temporarily unavailable, mimicking real-world situations where a driver’s view may
be obstructed or attention is momentarily diverted (Senders et al. 1967). This technique involves
deliberately blocking or occluding part of a driver’s visual field for short durations to assess their
ability to anticipate and react to changing traffic conditions when deprived of constant visual input
(e.g., Kujala & Sarkar 2024). This can be done by turning a display on and off or by physically
blocking a driver’s vision with a screen or eye goggles (Foley 2008). By replicating these real-world
scenarios in which visual input is momentarily lost—such as when drivers glance away from the
roadway to check a phone or dashboard display—researchers can examine how well drivers com-
pensate for the loss of visual information, estimate vehicle control, and predict potential hazards
when their view is blocked (Gilroy et al. 2019). This examination allows for insights into how
long drivers can safely manage their vehicle without updated visual cues (Foley 2008). The time

between occlusions is critical; if drivers can maintain control and make decisions during occlusion
periods, it suggests they have effectively processed and retained necessary visual information. The
technique also reveals how dependent drivers are on continuous visual input and how quickly they
can adapt to changes in visibility. For safety reasons, visual occlusion methods are often used in

driving performance (Kujala et al. 2023). In general, the technique provides insights into the min-

specific tasks (for a review, see Kujala et al. 2023).
Visual attention can also be assessed with simple stimulus response tasks in which drivers
respond when they detect a randomly presented visual stimulus in their forward line of sight.
For example, the detection response task (DRT) (ISO 2016) is a standardized protocol designed
to measure the cognitive demands associated with secondary-task engagement involving visual-
manual, voice-based, or haptic interfaces. In this task, participants are presented with a simple
stimulus (e.g., a light or vibrotactile device) every 3–5 s, and they must press a button when they
detect the stimulus. The DRT is highly sensitive to variations in cognitive load; as mental work-
load increases, reaction times to the DRT stimulus lengthen, and the likelihood of detecting the
stimulus (i.e., the hit rate) decreases (e.g., Biondi et al. 2024b, Castro et al. 2019). The LED, light
stimulus variant of the DRT provides a measure of visual attention (ISO 2016). This variation of
the DRT involves an LED positioned within the driver’s forward field of view, and participants are
instructed to press a finger switch whenever they detect the LED illuminating. Miss rates in re-
sponse to the LED provide a brief, accessible measure of visual attention, such that if the driver is
not looking at the forward roadway, they will not see the intermittently illuminated LED and thus
will miss the appropriate button response. Several studies have validated the method for its sensi-
tivity to visual task demands (e.g., Bruyas & Dumont 2013, Castro et al. 2016, Ranney et al. 2014,
van Winsum 2018) and changes in cognitive workload (Castro et al. 2016; ISO 2016; McDonnell
et al. 2021a,b; Strayer et al. 2018). However, while effective, this approach does not capture eyes-
off-road time and is less precise than eye tracking or glance coding, due to the intermittent onset
of the LED stimulus (typically every 3–5 s), which might not always align with moments when
drivers glance away from the road ahead.
Eye tracking can offer insights into how individuals process visual scenes and allocate visual
attention with high temporal resolution. Recording eye movements has been a key method for
understanding the visual nature of driving tasks (for reviews, see Crundall & Underwood 2011,
Grüner & Ansorge 2017, Kapitaniak et al. 2015). Driver-facing cameras offer a cost-effective and
portable solution, providing valuable insights into where and how drivers allocate their visual
attention in real time.
Fixation rate can be used to measure how long and frequently drivers focus on specific areas,
such as road signs or other vehicles, providing insights into which roadway regions and objects
are prioritized by drivers at any given moment. Saccades, or the rapid eye movements between
fixations, can be analyzed to assess how efficiently drivers scan their environment, indicating their
ability to quickly shift focus and detect potential hazards. Visual attention can also be assessed by
coding eye glances on and off the forward roadway. Off-road glances can be categorized as either
task relevant, such as looking at side mirrors, or task irrelevant, such as looking at a passenger,
cell phone, or the vehicle’s touchscreen (e.g., Biondi & Jajo 2024). Blink rate and duration have
also been used to detect driver workload (e.g., Benedetto et al. 2011, Yahoodik et al. 2021) and
drowsiness (Cooper et al. 2023, Shamini et al. 2022), both of which inevitably interact with visual
attention.
Gaze dispersion/concentration is also used to understand driver visual attention (Gonçalves
et al. 2020, Louw & Merat 2017, Wang et al. 2014). Gaze dispersion refers to the distribution of

a person’s visual attention across different areas of their visual field. It quantifies how widely or
narrowly a person’s gaze is spread while observing their environment. In the context of driving,
gaze dispersion indicates how drivers allocate their visual focus among various elements, such as
the road, other vehicles, traffic signs, and potential hazards. A wider gaze dispersion may suggest
divided attention, whereas a more concentrated gaze can indicate focused attention on relevant
driving tasks (Wang et al. 2014).
Electroencephalography (EEG) can also be used to measure a driver’s visual engagement with
their surroundings in real time (Lohani et al. 2019). EEG records brain activity via electrodes
positioned on the scalp, allowing for the direct recording of neural responses to environmental
demands with millisecond precision. Notably, EEG is noninvasive and can be made portable, mak-
ing it a practical tool for use in both driving simulators and on-road assessments. The oscillatory
activity that makes up the EEG signal can be decomposed into various frequency bands [e.g.,
theta (∼4–8 Hz), alpha (∼8–12 Hz), beta (∼12–30 Hz)] associated with different neurocognitive
functions (Lohani et al. 2019).
Alpha oscillations (8–12 Hz) distributed across the visual cortex (posterior regions of the scalp)
can be used to assess visual attention (Peylo et al. 2021, Romei et al. 2008, Sauseng et al. 2005,
Thut et al. 2006). Alpha oscillations are inversely related to visual attention, such that when visual
attention is high, alpha power is low, and when visual attention is low, alpha power is high (Foxe
& Snyder 2011, Toscani et al. 2010). Posterior alpha oscillations were among the earliest brain
rhythms identified in EEG research largely because of their robust response to visual engage-
ment; when the eyes are closed, alpha oscillations surge dramatically, highlighting their sensitivity
to changes in visual input (Adrian & Matthews 1934, Berger 1929, Goldman et al. 2002). Poste-
rior alpha activity has been used in driving contexts as a biomarker for driver visual engagement
with their environment (e.g., Brooks et al. 2018; McDonnell et al. 2021a,b, 2023). For example,
McDonnell and colleagues (2021a,b) recorded EEG activity while individuals drove on two unique
roadway conditions—a straight interstate and a winding, mountainous road. Drivers exhibited
significantly lower posterior alpha power on the curvy, mountainous road, indicating heightened
visual engagement in the more challenging driving demands associated with maintaining lane
position while navigating steep curves. Such work underscores the utility of posterior alpha oscil-
lations as a reliable biomarker for assessing visual attention in dynamic, real-world contexts. By
tracking changes in alpha power, researchers can gain valuable insights into how drivers respond
to varying levels of visual and cognitive demand on the road.
SPIDER 2.0
Our brains can process only a finite amount of incoming information at any given time, making it
difficult to attend to every detail in a dynamic environment (Kahneman 1973). As discussed, driv-
ing places exceptionally high demands on all our sensory systems, particularly vision, as drivers
must constantly filter and prioritize visual input while processing auditory,tactile,and spatial infor-
mation to make split-second decisions. Driver distraction arises from limited attentional capacity
(Strayer et al. 2022a,b). Safely operating a motor vehicle requires several mental processes, includ-
ing visually scanning the driving environment, predicting potential hazards—even those that are
not immediately visible—identifying objects within the line of sight, deciding whether an action is
necessary, and executing timely responses. Collectively, these processes are referred to as SPIDER
(Strayer & Fisher 2016).

Visual Scanning
Visually scanning the forward roadway, peripheral areas, rear and side mirrors, and vehicle blind
spots is essential for safe driving. This process aligns with Endsley’s level 1 situational awareness,
which involves perceiving the critical elements in the current environment that may affect driving
performance (Endsley 1995,2015).Engaging in a secondary task has been shown to decrease visual
scanning (Horrey et al. 2006; Mackenzie & Harris 2017; McCarley et al. 2001, 2004; Victor et al.
2005). Moreover, as visual scanning diminishes, drivers may develop tunnel vision, focusing only
on a narrow segment of the roadway, further hindering their situational awareness and ability to
anticipate potential threats (van Winsum 2019).
Even secondary tasks that do not have an explicit visual component can lead to drivers focusing
their gaze on the center of the driving scene (Briggs et al. 2017, Engström et al. 2005, Goh et al.
2021, Harbluk et al. 2007, He et al. 2011, Recarte & Nunes 2000, Reimer 2009, Reimer et al.
2012, Strayer et al. 2017, Tsai et al. 2007, Victor et al. 2005). This concentration of gaze may also
affect lateral lane position variability (Readinger et al. 2002, Rogers et al. 2005, Wilson et al. 2008),

Hazard Prediction
The dual mechanisms of control framework developed by Braver and colleagues (e.g., Braver
2012; Braver et al. 2008, 2009) identifies two modes of cognitive control: reactive and proactive.
Reactive control is a bottom-up process that functions as a late-correction strategy in response
to unexpected events, such as when a pedestrian suddenly steps out in front of a parked vehicle.
In contrast, proactive control is a top-down process that operates anticipatorily to guide driving
behavior. Hazard prediction, a critical component of safe driving, exemplifies proactive control
and aligns with Endsley’s level 3 situational awareness, which focuses on predicting future states
of the environment (Endsley 1995, 2015).
Anticipatory glances toward specific areas of the roadway indicate that a driver is making top-
down predictions about potential hazards in those locations. For instance, a driver may glance
at an alleyway as they approach to ensure that no vehicles will pull out in front of them. Fisher
(2015) studied drivers’ eye movements at intersections and found that they often make an initial
glance to the left and right as they approach and then a secondary glance just before entering the
intersection. Multitasking has been shown to reduce these anticipatory glances toward potential
hazards (Biondi et al. 2015; Ebadi et al. 2019; T. Taylor, L. Roman, K. McFeaters, M. Romoser,
A. Borowsky, et al., unpublished data). Biondi et al. (2015) demonstrated that the likelihood of
glancing for pedestrians in crosswalks diminished when drivers were engaged in a cognitively de-
manding secondary task. Beyond hazard prediction at intersections, Ebadi et al. (2019) replicated
this pattern at work zones and marked midblock crosswalks. Similarly, T. Taylor, L. Roman, K.
McFeaters, M. Romoser, A. Borowsky, et al. (unpublished data) found that secondary-task engage-
ment caused experienced drivers to behave more like inexperienced drivers. This claim is further
supported by Wright et al. (2016), who found that experienced drivers look more frequently at
potential hazard locations than younger drivers do. Additionally, multitasking can induce a form
of contextual blindness, characterized by impairments in the ability to use environmental cues to
guide attention in the driving environment (Cooper & Strayer 2025). This contextual blindness
hinders both the acquisition of new sequences and the execution of previously learned tasks.
Identification
People can look at their surroundings but still fail to see important information, resulting in what
is known as look but fail to see (LBFTS) errors (Wolfe et al. 2022). These errors arise from the
limited capacity of attention; observers must actively focus on what they are looking at or risk being
blind to it (Mack & Rock 1998, Simons 2000, Simons & Chabris 1999). A classic study by Simons
& Chabris (1999) illustrates this phenomenon: Participants tasked with counting the number of
passes made by a basketball team failed to notice a person in a gorilla suit walking through the
scene. The gorilla stopped, faced the camera, thumped its chest, and then exited, remaining visible
for over 5 s. Remarkably, about half of the participants did not see it, demonstrating inattentional
blindness (LBFTS). These errors are prevalent in driving situations (Herslund & Jorgensen 2002)
and become more frequent when individuals are multitasking (Saryazdi et al. 2019, Strayer &
Cooper 2015).
Drivers must attend to visual input to identify objects in their environment, enabling them
to respond appropriately and promptly. Identification, or seeing, is crucial for safe driving and
relates to Endsley’s level 2 situational awareness, which involves comprehension of the current

situation (Endsley 1995, 2015). However, drivers engaged in secondary tasks often experience

Strayer et al. (2003) examined drivers’ recognition memory for objects they looked at while
driving (as verified by eye tracking) and found that those talking on a cell phone exhibited a 50%
reduction in recognition memory compared to baseline performance. In subsequent studies, these
researchers varied the safety relevance of objects in the driving scene (e.g., pedestrians, other ve-
hicles, parked cars, billboards) to assess whether more-safety-relevant items were less susceptible
to inattentional blindness. Unfortunately, they found no correlation between safety relevance and
vulnerability and inattentional blindness (Strayer et al. 2004).
While multitasking, drivers fail to strategically prioritize the processing of safety-critical in-
formation in the driving environment over the conversation on their cell phones. Supporting
evidence for inattentional blindness comes from event-related potential (ERP) studies using EEG.
ERPs are deflections in the EEG waveform that occur in response to a discrete event in the
driving environment. The amplitude of the P300 indexes the amount of attention allocated to
a stimulus—the higher the amplitude is, the higher the attention allocated (Sirevaag et al. 1989).
Research has shown that the amplitude of the P300 component—elicited by discrete driving-
relevant events—decreases as secondary task demands increase (e.g., Leynes et al. 2018; Strayer &
Drews 2007b; Strayer et al. 2004, 2015). This ERP pattern indicates that engagement in secondary
tasks compromises the encoding and registration of traffic-related information.
Posterior alpha oscillations in the brain may also serve as an index of inattentional blindness.
As discussed above, alpha oscillations distributed across the visual cortex (posterior regions of the
scalp) can be used to assess visual attention at the wheel (e.g., McDonnell et al. 2021b, 2023).
Given that increased power in posterior alpha oscillations is associated with decreased visual at-
tention (Peylo et al. 2021) and that a driver’s ability to process peripheral visual information is
diminished during secondary-task engagement, posterior alpha power is a promising metric for
indicating momentary threats to inattentional blindness. The use of posterior alpha power as an
indicator of inattentional blindness has been established by researchers in contexts beyond driving
(Hutchinson et al.2020,2021),suggesting its promising applicability to driving scenarios.By mon-
itoring posterior alpha activity, researchers can gain insights into the driver’s attentional state and
identify moments when critical visual information may be overlooked, enhancing our understand-
ing of the neural mechanisms underlying inattentional blindness in real-world driving scenarios.
Decision-Making
Drivers must assess multiple sources of information to make safe decisions when changing lanes,
merging into traffic, or making left turns across oncoming vehicles. However, the ability to make
sound judgments declines with age (Strayer 2015). For instance, multivehicle crashes at inter-
sections account for 20–25% of fatal accidents among drivers under the age of 60. In contrast,
the crash rate steadily increases after age 60, reaching 45% of fatal crashes by age 85. Intersec-
tions pose a significant mental challenge, requiring drivers to focus on traffic signals, cross traffic,
pedestrians, and other vulnerable road users (NSC 2018). Meanwhile, the rates of single-vehicle
crashes and multivehicle crashes outside intersections continue to decline with age. Older adults
face heightened risks, especially when tasked with processing multiple sources of information,
particularly during left turns at intersections (Rolison et al. 2018, Sifrit et al. 2011).

a safe gap in the flow of vehicles to complete the maneuver. Although dedicated left-turn signals

Moreover, multitasking can impair drivers’ ability to accurately evaluate critical information

gaps and the speed of oncoming vehicles, a trend that was particularly pronounced on wet road-

maintained a narrower safety margin compared to their nondistracted counterparts.
The lane change task (ISO 2010,Zheng et al.2023) is a standardized protocol designed to assess
the impact of secondary tasks on driver decision-making. In this protocol, participants drive in the
middle lane of a three-lane highway, where traffic in adjacent lanes is moving at varying speeds.
When a decelerating vehicle is detected ahead—as done by collision warning systems found in
modern vehicles—drivers must quickly assess the situation, factoring in the speed and position of
other vehicles, and decide whether to safely switch to the right or left lane to avoid a potential
collision (Zheng et al. 2023). This is an ecologically valid task that has been extensively used to
explore driver decision-making capabilities under various driving conditions (e.g., Gonçalves et al.
2022, Wang et al. 2018). It draws on both foveal and peripheral visual processing, testing driver
visual attention during complex decision-making tasks.
Execution of a Response
One of the key indicators of divided attention is a slowing of reaction time (Posner & Boies
1971, Posner & Keele 1967). When a driving situation necessitates an evasive action—like swerv-
ing to avoid a child running into the street or braking to prevent a collision with an oncoming
vehicle—these reactions are often delayed (Atchley et al. 2017; Caird et al. 2008, 2018; Haque &
Washington 2014; Horrey et al. 2006; Ishigami & Klein 2009). The impact of secondary tasks be-
comes more pronounced as the perceptual demands of the driving environment increase, such as
when traffic volume rises (Strayer et al. 2003). Additionally, engaging in a secondary task like a cell
phone conversation skews reaction time distributions, such that the late responses are particularly
delayed (Ratcliff & Strayer 2014). Research by Brown et al. (2001) found that these delayed brake
reactions significantly elevate both the likelihood and the severity of motor vehicle crashes.
SITUATIONAL AWARENESS
A motorist’s situational awareness is shaped by the SPIDER-related processes illustrated in
Figure 1. The degradation of situational awareness is influenced by both the mental and the
temporal demands of the secondary task being performed, with more significant impacts resulting
from longer and more demanding tasks.
In Figure 1, the bidirectional arrows connecting scanning, predicting, and identifying to sit-
uational awareness correspond to Endsley’s (1995) three levels of situational awareness. Level 1
involves the perception of elements in the current situation, level 2 pertains to the comprehension
of that situation, and level 3 relates to the prediction of its future status. These bidirectional links
signify that scanning, predicting, and identifying continually update the driver’s mental model,
which in turn informs the allocation of attention to each of these processes (Kahneman 1973).
The fidelity of a motorist’s situational awareness of the driving situation directly influ-
ences their decision-making and the speed of their responses. Endsley (1995) found that
even minor lapses in situational awareness can lead to poor performance. Additionally,

can persist for over 30 s after the secondary task has ended. This lingering distraction represents
a hidden cost of multitasking. Therefore, the impact of performing a functionally independent
secondary task on a driver’s situational awareness extends across the interval from t0 to t2.

CONCLUSIONS
Motor vehicle crashes are the leading cause of accidental injury deaths in the United States
(NHTSA 2015). According to the National Motor Vehicle Crash Causation Survey, 94% of
crashes on US roadways are attributed to human error (Singh 2015). Recognition errors, including
driver inattention, distraction, and inadequate surveillance, account for 41% of crashes. Decision
errors, such as driving too fast for conditions, making illegal maneuvers, and misjudging gaps or
other vehicles’ speeds, contribute to 33% of crashes. Performance errors, like lateral or longitu-
dinal control, account for 11%, while nonperformance errors, such as fatigue or falling asleep at
the wheel, make up 7%. Other driver errors account for the remaining 8%.
Most of these crashes are preventable and stem from failures in SPIDER-related processes.2
As illustrated in Figure 1, a driver’s allocation of attention to these fundamental tasks is one of
the most significant factors influencing roadway safety. When drivers fail to dedicate adequate
attention to driving, they are more prone to making mistakes that lead to crashes, injuries, and
fatalities.
Driving provides an excellent opportunity to examine visual attention in an everyday context,
where multitasking can, and often does, have adverse consequences. The task of driving provides
an important context in which to demonstrate if, how, and when the basic science on attention and
multitasking scales from the laboratory to the real world. The so-called scaling problem refers to
the difficulty in applying the findings from controlled laboratory studies to larger, more complex
real-world situations (e.g., Nature Editorial 2019). Here we show that the controlled laboratory
studies of attention and multitasking in driving scale well: Even small decreases in object detection
and identification and slight delays in responding can cascade into an increase in crashes and
fatalities on the roadway.
SUMMARY POINTS
1. Driving is a complex multisensory task, with visual attention playing a critical role in a
motorist’s situational awareness. Multitasking degrades this awareness, leading to missed
and delayed reactions to roadway hazards.
2. Engaging in secondary tasks, such as cell phone use, diverts attention from the driving
task. Sources of distractions include visual (looking away), manual (hands off the wheel),
and cognitive (mental focus).
3. Safe driving relies on visual scanning, hazard prediction, identification of objects in the
line of sight, good decision-making, and timely execution of a response (SPIDER). Dis-
tractions degrade these processes, leading to diminished situational awareness and an
increased risk of crashes.
4. Multitasking can cause inattentional blindness, where drivers fail to see objects in plain
sight. Engaging in secondary tasks also slows responses to unexpected road hazards.
The effect persists even after the distraction ends, delaying the recovery of situational
awareness.
5. Distractions impair the ability to judge safe gaps in traffic, make lane changes, and
respond quickly in emergencies, increasing accident risks, especially at intersections.

FUTURE ISSUES
1. How does frequent multitasking affect long-term driving behavior and skill reten-
tion? Are habitual multitasking drivers less attentive, even when they are not actively
distracted?
2. Do some drivers handle distractions better than other drivers? How do factors like age,
experience, cognitive abilities, and personality traits influence distraction-related crash
risk?
3. How do drivers adapt to new in-vehicle technologies and driver assistance systems?
Do automated systems that reduce cognitive load inadvertently increase reliance on
secondary tasks (e.g., texting, browsing)?
4. Most research focuses on cell phone use, but how do newer distractions (e.g.,
voice assistants, infotainment systems, augmented reality dashboards) affect driving
performance?
5. How does the complexity of a driving environment (urban versus rural, high-traffic ver-
sus low-traffic) interact with different types of distractions? Is there a threshold beyond
which distractions become overwhelmingly dangerous, regardless of a driver’s skill level?
6. Can brain-based measures (e.g., EEG, functional magnetic resonance imaging) be used
to predict distraction-related accidents in real-world settings? Can biometrics such as
heart rate variability or eye tracking effectively predict when a driver is too distracted to
react safely?
7. Does partial automation (e.g., adaptive cruise control, lane-keeping assist) affect driver
attention and response times? What are the potential risks of overreliance on au-
tomation, and how do drivers manage the transition between automated and manual
driving?
