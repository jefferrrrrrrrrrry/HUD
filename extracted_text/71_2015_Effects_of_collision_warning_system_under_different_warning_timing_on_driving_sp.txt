Abstract—The study examined impacts of different releasing 
time of warning with an intersection collision warning system 
(ICWS) on driver behavior. Forty five participants drove a 
simulated vehicle through five driving scenes containing seven 
different unexpected collision events with different releasing time 
of warning and no warning (control) group was presented to 
counter balanced with the collision event types. Comparing with 
no warning group, speed trends had significant differences under 
different releasing time of warning conditions in this study. The 
results also indicated that releasing ICWS warnings could reduce 
driving speed to a hazard event so that drivers would adjust 
speed more appropriately to respond to collision situation. 
Considering the time of application of violent deceleration for 
different releasing time of warning, 3.0 s to 4.0 s before a collision 
occurred might be a proper time to provide warning. In terms of 
distance to conflict point with maximum deceleration, different 
releasing time of warning showed statistically different, which 
suggested that drivers would reserve more space to clear 
impending collision with earlier warning. Additionally, with the 
time of warning released earlier, the time of the maximum 
deceleration rate appeared earlier. Better designs of effective 
warning when driving at critical intersections could be derived 
from these results. 
Keywords—Collision warning system; Auditory warning; 
Releasing time of warning; Driving speed; 
I.  INTRODUCTION  
Safe driving at intersections was one of the most dynamic 
and complex tasks for drivers on road, and a high degree of 
visual and spatial processing was required [1-2] in this task. 
According to in-depth analyses of accidents, recognition error 
was one of the main contributing factors for accidents at 
intersections [3], which had found that lack of information 
was the main reason leading to an accident in above 90% of 
intersection accidents. The similar result was showed in the 
research [4]. In most cases, the other traffic participants on the 
road were not noticed by the drivers and they even failed to 
yield to the other road users with priority.. Thus, in-vehicle 
warning information systems were installed for the purpose of 
enhancing drivers’ safety and it could be also used to help 
drivers deal with the complex situation at intersections.  
Due to advances in in-vehicle collision warning system 
technology, driver's safety benefits a lot from the warnings. 
Several simulator and on-road studies suggested that the 
warning could produce several potential bene¿ts, such as 
reducing reaction time to hazardous situations [5-8], reducing 
collision involvements[9-11]. Additionally, it was well-known 
that the effectiveness of collision warning system warnings 
was affected by various parameters [12-16]. A key, of course, 
was that the released warnings information must improve 
collision warning system effectively if they are to be useful. A 
study [17] had indicated that the auditory warning information 
could assist drivers in detecting the RLR vehicles in a timely 
manner, and early warning provided drivers more adequate 
time and space to decelerate to avoid collisions with the 
conflicting vehicle. 
The studies mentioned above indicated that the drivers 
could greatly benefit from the auditory warning on the 
direction of the risk in driving. To date, there had been few 
promising studies on analyzing the effect of different warning 
releasing time  on drivers’ behavior [18-19]. Therefore, more 
research were needed to further determine the proper releasing 
time. Experiments were conducted to evaluate the effect of 
different warning releasing time on driving performance 
including driving speed trend and distance to conflict point.

II. METHODS 
A. Participants and apparatus  
A total of 45 subjects (53% males) participated in the 
study. Participants ranged from 31 to 39 years of age (mean 
age 35 years). With regard to their driving experience, all 
participants had a driver’s license for at least 3 years and 
drove more than twenty thousand kilometers per year. All 
participants had normal or corrected to normal vision and 
hearing. 
In this study, BJTU Driving Simulator developed by Real 
Time Company was used to present the simulated driving 
scenarios and ICWS warnings. The simulator integrates a real 
vehicle (a Ford Focus), a virtual reality-based visual and audio 
system, one degree of freedom motion platform which can 
imitate the feeling of motion and a host computer system to 
simulate the virtual environment of an urban road. Inside the 
driving cabin, the simulator is equipped with full operational 
control including a steering wheel, brake and accelerator pedal 
as well as turn signal indicators, etc. Also, the driving 
simulator has a 300-degree front view of display system with 
simulated back mirrors. Side-view screens and a rear-view 
screen allow the driver to monitor traffic from all directions.  
B. Driving scenario and Experiment design  
In order to examine the effectiveness of the ICWS 
warnings with different releasing time in a critical intersection, 
a situation with a high collision probability was required. 
Studies had mentioned that red light running crashes were 
found to be more severe than other types of crashes, and other 
accidents studies had shown similar results [20-21]. Thus, a 
Red-light-running (RLR) collision scenario in this experiment 
was defined as conflict event at an experimental intersection. 
When drivers entered the experimental intersection, the traffic 
light for simulator was green, meanwhile, the RLR vehicle 
attempted to negotiate a red light at the same intersection. In 
order to avoid the collision, the driver had to decelerate.  
Fig 1 showed the specific RLR crash scenario designed at 
the experimental intersection in the experiment. TTC sensor 
was selected to calculate the simulator’s approaching time to 
the conflict point in real time and to trigger the RLR vehicle, 
proximity sensor was used to trigger auditory warning. The 
RLR vehicle was trigged to cross the intersection with a 
velocity of 20 m/s at point A by the TTC sensor when the 
simulator was 7 s away from the conflict point at point B. An 
auditory warning will be not be published to alert the driver 
until the approaching time to the experimental intersection 
reached a pre-designed releasing time. 
The main factor in this experiment was releasing time of 
ICWS warnings. In this study, the latest releasing time of 
warning should at least allow drivers to react to the warning 
and the earliest releasing time of warning could not increase 
user's distrust of warning, thus, seven kinds of releasing time 
of warning that varied from 2.5 s to 5.5 s before collision 
occurred with 0.5 s interval was set. With no warning as 
control group, eight experimental intersections were designed 
for each participant, containing seven potential collision event 
paired with different releasing time of  ICWS warnings and 
the control group. 
In the experiment, the eight experimental intersection were 
randomly distributed in five similar driving scene. Each 
driving scene would last about 5 min to complete, which 
consisted of two-way two-lane urban roadway that 3.5 m wide 
and the speed limit was 80 km/h. Six signalized intersections 
were located every 800-1200m in a driving scene, and one or 
two experimental intersection was randomly assigned to the 
six similar intersections in each driving scene, so that drivers 
could not predict the conflict event according to location or 
the temporal order in memory during the experiment 
operation. Therefore, all of ICWS warnings were presented in 
the driving simulations, and individual drivers only 
encountered each ICWS warning once and only drove each 
scene and scenario once.

C. Procedure and data analysis  
For the study, all participants first provided their personal 
information, including sex, age, licensure status, and 
experience with video games. Before the formal experiment, 
all participants were given experimental instruction for the 
driving task and the subjects were instructed in how to operate 
the simulator, including driving straightly, accelerating, 
decelerating, left/right turning, and then they were given a 10-
15min driving practice to adapt the operation of simulator. 
During each scenario, the drivers could take a rest of 10 min. 
Besides, the drivers could quit the experiment at any time if 
they feel any motion sickness, and their unfinished 
experiments data would be deleted from the dataset. At the 
end of the experiment, the drivers had to finish a questionnaire 
about the subjective effectiveness to ICWS warnings by 
participants themselves. Besides, they could get financial 
compensation for the time taken to complete the experiment. 
In this study, the driving speed and distance to the conflict 
point were used to evaluate driving behaviors when 
encountered potential collisions, which could be obtained by 
the driving simulator in real-time. Paired T-test was utilized to 
examine group differences among speed and location in 
different ICWS warnings conditions, especially on the 
different releasing time of warning. Analysis of variance 
(ANOVA) was also used to examine differences among

III. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 
A. Driving Speed  
When drivers were gradually approaching signalized 
intersection, they would be more cautious to adjust the speed. 
As is shown in Figure 2, during the early stage of potential 
collision events (6.5 s to 5.5 s to conflict point), drivers were 
approaching the intersection with no obvious speed change, 
which might be caused by a low traffic flow and green phase 
ahead. During the middle stage of releasing warning (5.5 s to 0 
s to conflict point), drivers started to decelerate at different 
time distance to the conflict point under different releasing 
time of warning. Additionally, the last part of this stage can 
indicate results of collision avoidance measures under 
different releasing time of warning.  
The speed change trend of no warning condition was 
shown in Figure 2-a, the speed slowed down slightly until time 
distance to conflict point was 1.5 s, when the drivers intensify 
the strength on brake pedal. However, such behavior was 
found to correspond with a high crash rate (44.1%). But for 
warning conditions, the trend of decelerating was more 
obvious than that of no warning, which indicated that warning 
could reduce speed when drivers crossed an intersection and 
indirectly reflected that warning system can enhance driving 
safety. Similar results were also identified by previous studies 
[1, 12]. When ICWS warning was provided 2.5 s (see Figure 
2-b) before collision, the crash rate was second high (29.4%) 
and there was no violent deceleration. For warning provided 
3.0 s (see Figure 2-c) before collision, sharp deceleration 
appeared at 2.5 s before the conflict point. For warning 
provided 3.5 s (see Figure 2-d) before collision, more obvious 
deceleration was adopted at the time distance of 1.5 s to the 
conflict point. For warning provided 4.0 s (see Figure 2-e) 
before collision, sharp deceleration was adopted at the time 
distance of 2.5 s to the conflict point, and the same situation 
occurred when the warning was provided 4.5 s (see Figure 2-f) 
before collision. For warning provided 5.0 s (see Figure 2-g) 
before collision, violent decelerations were adopted at 3.5 s 
before the conflict point, which was nearly the same as the 
condition in releasing time of 5.5 s (see Figure 2-h).

B. Distance to conflict point with maximum deceleration  
At the signalized intersection, if a RLR crash develops, the 
conflict point or area could be determined. When drivers 
pressed the brake pedal to reach the maximum value, the 
distance to the conflict point was calculated and analyzed by 
ANOVA, which was an important indicator to show safe 
distance to a potential collision.  
Table 2 had listed the drivers’ distance to conflict point at 
maximum deceleration rate under different warning releasing 
time. Experimental results indicated that the mean distance 
without warning information (33.24 m) was smaller than those 
of with warning information (36.57 m, 44.44 m, 36.92 m, 
50.07 m, 50.02 m, 56.26 m, 59.49 m), indicating that the 
different ICWS releasing time of warnings made a significant 
difference on the driver’s safety distance (p<0.05) to the 
conflict vehicle. The results showed that the warning with 
earlier releasing time could increase distance to conflict point 
when the deceleration rate reached maximum. The possible 
reason was that with earlier warning, drivers could respond to 
a potential collision earlier so that they could reserve more 
space to eliminate the emergent event or  other risk.

IV. CONCLUSIONS 
This study mainly investigated drivers’ speed trend and 
distance to conflict point at maximum deceleration rate under 
different ICWS releasing time of warning conditions. Previous 
research had showned that, firstly, compared to no warning, 
drivers slowed down to make a decision to brake after 
listening the auditory warning, which demonstrated that 
releasing an ICWS warning could be expected to provide 
some help in reducing driving speed to the hazard event. That 
was to say, warning information was presented to impact the 
adjustment of speed to respond appropriately to the RLR 
collision situation, and the results presented a trend of earlier 
violent deceleration and longer distance to conflict point when 
releasing warning in the critical intersection situation.  
According to the results of driving speed and distance to 
the conflict point under different releasing time of warning 
conditions, warning should be released at least 3.0 s before a 
collision at a signalized intersection, and considering the time 
of application of violent deceleration for different releasing 
time of warning, 3.0 s to 4.0 s before a collision occurred 
might be a proper time to provide warning. Additionally, the 
time of the maximum deceleration rate  appeared earlier as the 
time of warning was released earlier, and drivers would 
reserve more room to clear impending collision to response 
more safely and comfortable with the earlier warning. Overall, 
the results in this study have a mutual promotion with the 
existing body of literature in revealing that how the different 
releasing time of warnings affects drivers’ performance at the 
critical intersections.
