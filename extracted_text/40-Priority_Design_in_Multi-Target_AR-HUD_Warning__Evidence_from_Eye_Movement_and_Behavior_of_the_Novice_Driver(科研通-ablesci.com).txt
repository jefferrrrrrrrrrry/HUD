ABSTRACT 
Technically, Augmented Reality Head-Up Display (AR-HUD) technology can augment multiple tar­
gets in the scene. However, existing literature predominantly focuses on scenarios involving sin­
gle-target augmentation. The understanding of multi-target augmentation and studies exploring 
effective presentation methods under such conditions are notably scarce. This study evaluates the 
efficacy of integrating color-based warning priority design across multi-target scenarios. 45 
Participants in different warning modes (Equivalent, Hierarchical, and Baseline) view AR-aug­
mented driving videos and respond to risky targets. Their behavioral performance and eye-track­
ing data are compared. Findings indicate that the equivalent warning mode, lacking in priority 
design, adversely affects driver performance, prolongs reaction times, and elevates saccade counts, 
and gaze entropy. Conversely, the hierarchical warning mode significantly ameliorates driver reac­
tion times and the time to first fixation, while also reducing saccade counts and gaze entropy, 
demonstrating the efficacy of the warning priority design. The findings provide insight into the 
design of AR-HUD with multi-target augmentation.

1. Introduction
Vehicles equipped with Augmented Reality Head-up Display 
(AR-HUD) technology are increasingly entering the market 
(Coleman Merenda et al., 2018). AR-HUD is an advanced 
driving assistance system that integrates real and virtual ele­
ments within the driver’s line of sight (Karatas et al., 2020), 
facilitating the direct perception of artificial information 
superimposed on physical reality (Kim et al., 2016). A key 
advantage of AR-HUD is its ability to align virtual warnings 
with the direction and location of hazards (Yontem et al., 
2021), communicate spatially relevant information about 
potential dangers (Currano et al., 2021), direct drivers’ 
attention to critical situations (Kim et al., 2016; 2018), and 
enhance safety by avoiding potential risks without diverting 
the driver’s gaze from the road (Herbert et al., 2017; Minh 
Tien et al., 2016). Studies have demonstrated the effective­
ness of AR warnings, such as improving situational and spa­
tial awareness (e.g., Kim & Hwang, 2016; Ng-Thow-Hing 
et al., 2013) and reducing response times (e.g., Wang et al., 
2023), through visual cues.
However, most of these studies focus on scenarios with a 
single risky target (Wu et al., 2022), while more complex sit­
uations involving multiple road users and objects (i.e., 
multi-target scenarios) are rarely addressed. In reality, driv­
ing often involves a multitude of objects in the scene (Faure 
et al., 2016), and the typical semantic segmentation used in 
Advanced Driver Assistance Systems (ADAS) assumes that 
all objects are equally important and aims to segment them 
accurately (Chen et al., 2019). Based on existing detection 
algorithms, such as Deep Convolutional Neural Networks 
(DCNNs), AR-HUD systems would augment all target infor­
mation detected by vehicle sensors and analytical systems 
(Currano et al., 2021). Current prototype interfaces for in- 
vehicle AR-HUDs also predominantly utilize multi-target 
augmentation. It is conceivable that future AR-HUD interfa­
ces will augment multiple targets in the driving environ­
ment, including pedestrians, vehicles, traffic lights, and street 
signs within a certain range, rather than focusing solely on 
risky targets.
Compared to single-target augmentation, multi-target 
augmentation could have a significantly different impact on 
drivers’ visual patterns and situational awareness. While it 
may aid in focusing attention on relevant driving-related 
targets, it could also distract drivers and potentially diminish 
the effectiveness of AR warnings. In particular, novice driv­
ers often have difficulty scanning the area ahead effectively 
(Underwood et al., 2003) and have limited attentional 
resources (van Leeuwen et al., 2015), so multi-target aug­
mentation may have a more pronounced effect on them. It 
is crucial to explore the potential trade-offs of multi-target 
augmentation and to investigate how to effectively present

1.1. Multi-target augmentation in AR-HUD and potential 
impacts
The integration of AR-HUD with multi-target augmentation 
may have a profound influence on drivers’ visual attention 
and situational awareness. On the one side, the multi-target 
augmentation in AR-HUD can, to a certain extent, help 
drivers allocate their visual attention to recognize numerous 
targets in the scene. Specifically, driving is inherently a com­
plex activity, necessitating the perception of various traffic 
targets (Chung et al., 2022; Herbert et al., 2017). 
Pedestrians, vehicles, and traffic signals (e.g., road signs and 
traffic lights), are crucial targets for aiding in the compre­
hension of road conditions (Chen et al., 2019; Herbert et al., 
2017). A primary task for drivers is to detect, recognize, and 
allocate attention to these pertinent targets (Arexis et al., 
2017; Colley et al., 2021). Neglecting to do so can lead to 
oversight of route details or even collisions with other 
vehicles or pedestrians (Luo et al., 2008). Scholars have pro­
posed that the user interface of Advanced Driver Assistance 
Systems (ADAS) should alert drivers to significant signs and 
obstacles by sensing external scene targets (Kastner et al., 
2011; Wu et al., 2022). Consequently, the integration of AR- 
HUD with object annotation technology serves to enhance 
the visual perception of most targets within a specific area 
(Cavanagh & Alvarez, 2005; Ohn-Bar & Trivedi, 2017). By 
emphasizing relevant objects and guiding the gaze in a man­
ner that respects the process of information acquisition 
(Eyraud et al., 2015), this approach facilitates drivers’ per­
ception of scene elements. Prior research has already proto­
typed an AR-HUD interface that highlights traffic objects 
like vehicles, pedestrians, and road signs, confirming its effi­
cacy in enhancing situational awareness (Colley et al., 2021).
However, multi-target augmentation in AR-HUD interfa­
ces could also negatively impact visual attention and situ­
ational awareness. Human attention capacity is inherently 
limited (Lee et al., 2024), and it is not feasible to attend to 
every simultaneous target (Broadbent, 1957; Sanocki et al., 
2015). Multiple targets may compete for attention, increase 
the cognitive load (Merenda et al., 2018; Zhang & Lee, 
2021), and hinder the allocation of attention to critical tar­
gets (Currano et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2024). Recognizing crit­
ical objects surrounded by numerous objects can be 
challenging (Albonico et al., 2018; Chaney et al., 2014; Levi, 
2008). Traditional psychological studies have shown that the 
presence of nearby objects affects the identification of criti­
cal objects (Xia et al., 2020), and the perception of a target 
can be significantly compromised in the presence of similar 
flanking elements (Chicherov & Herzog, 2015; Levi, 2008; 
Pichlmeier & Pfeiffer, 2021; Sanocki et al., 2015). When 
employing multiple AR graphics to augment various targets 
in the driving environment, such as pedestrians, vehicles, 
traffic signals, and road signs, these graphics act as multiple 
information sources. While AR-HUD is designed to capture 
attention, an increase in the number of augmented targets 
could exacerbate attention competition. For instance, Muela 
et al. (2021), using two bounding boxes to simultaneously 
augment two types of hazards (i.e., potential and developing 
hazards), found that the presence of dual targets significantly 
impaired drivers’ performance. Similarly, Kim et al. (2016) 
augmented all detected pedestrians within the sensor’s field 
and observed that drivers needed to exert additional effort 
to discern dangerous pedestrians among cluttered bounding 
boxes. It has been suggested that an overload of information 
in the line of sight could impair driving performance 
(Tretten et al., 2009). In particular, there is a consistent dis­
crepancy in distance between the projected AR graphics and 
real-world objects when utilizing an AR-HUD. The distance 
of AR graphics remains constant, whereas real-world objects 
are situated at varying distances. This discrepancy can 
impact the eye’s optical power, leading to an accommoda­
tion-convergence conflict (Kang et al., 2019, Ng-Thow-Hing 
et al., 2013). Prior research indicates that accommodation- 
convergence conflict correlates with visual fatigue (Shibata 
et al., 2011) and diminished attentiveness (Kang et al., 
2019). For instance, Kang et al. (2019) demonstrated that 
the distance disparity between AR images and real objects 
affects the accuracy of object recognition. Consequently, we 
hypothesize that when viewing multiple AR-augmented 
graphics and external objects simultaneously, a driver’s vis­
ual attention may be more significantly compromised than 
when a single target is augmented, potentially increasing the 
difficulty in recognizing critical targets.
Overall, while some studies have examined the impact of 
multiple AR graphics on visual attention and shown that an 
increase in AR graphics can lead to perceived visual com­
plexity (Currano et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2024), they have 
largely focused on static screen-fixed graphics. It has been 
suggested that dynamic stimuli may result in greater per­
formance decrements than static stimuli in visual vigilance 
tasks (Koelega et al., 1990). Given the dynamic and world- 
fixed graphical nature of multi-target augmentation, these 
dynamic AR graphics may have more pronounced effects on 
visual attention than static AR graphics. Furthermore, in 
dynamic driving contexts, the application of multi-target 
augmentation, which includes pedestrians, vehicles, road 
signs, and traffic lights, may impact drivers’ perception of 
the significance of these environmental elements and their 
overall situational awareness. Particularly, when environ­
mental cues are ambiguous, individuals are required to per­
form diagnostic procedures to ascertain the underlying 
issues (Orasanu et al., 1993). The potential blurring effect of 
such augmentation on situational awareness warrants further 
investigation.
1.2. Priority design in multi-target augmentation 
condition
The preceding discussion suggests that multi-target augmen­
tation in AR-HUD can be a double-edged sword, particu­
larly for novice drivers. Novice drivers often struggle with 
identifying traffic targets (Koelega et al., 1990; van Leeuwen 
et al., 2015), making multi-target augmentation a potentially

essential aid. However, these drivers also have limited spare 
attentional resources (Duncan et al., 1991; van Leeuwen 
et al., 2015), which may exacerbate the visual attention com­
petition effects associated with multi-target augmentation. 
Moreover, to a certain extent, the similarity in multi-target 
augmentation can blur the significance of these pieces of 
information. Without discerning the significance of various 
pieces of information, individuals might erroneously believe 
they are focusing on the most critical data (Endsley & 
Endsley, 1995). In particular, novices may acquire less infor­
mation due to their inability to differentiate between similar 
inputs (Endsley & Endsley, 1995). Therefore, while sufficient 
AR warnings were present in this condition, the difficulty in 
determining their relevance to important goals ultimately 
makes it difficult to focus on the most important one 
promptly as well. Since situational awareness encompasses a 
comprehensive understanding of all elements (Endsley & 
Endsley, 1995; Teichmann & Motus, 2021), this could affect 
drivers’ comprehension of the dynamic driving environment. 
Improperly arranged additional information could over­
shadow its potential benefits, especially in interfaces that 
must support time-critical tasks (Beck et al., 2010). To 
ensure the effectiveness of AR-HUD for the majority of 
drivers, including novice drivers, we focus on exploring an 
effective interface design from the perspective of multi-target 
augmentation. We propose integrating the concept of warn­
ing priority with multi-augmentation.
The Intelligent Transport Systems (ITS) of the United 
Nations Economic Commission for Europe (UNECE) has 
clearly defined the concept of warning priority as the estab­
lishment of relative importance among multiple pieces of 
information and their ranking in the presentation emphasis. 
It is undeniable that not all targets in the driving environ­
ment are equally important (Chen et al., 2019; Ohn-Bar & 
Trivedi, 2017). Wu et al. (2022) has delineated the impor­
tance levels of driving-related targets based on the risk they 
pose to driving, advocating for a high importance level for 
targets that present a direct crash risk or induce serious traf­
fic violations, and a medium importance level for those that 
influence future driving decisions but do not pose an imme­
diate crash risk. For instance, a road sign indicating a turn 
is considered a medium-importance target; it may necessi­
tate a prompt response from the driver, but missing this sig­
nal does not necessarily result in dangerous consequences. 
Multi-target augmentation involves world-fixed graphics, 
which closely tie the nature of the warnings to the nature of 
the targets themselves, facilitating the establishment of warn­
ing priorities in the AR-HUD interface. Prior research has 
suggested that advanced driver assistance systems should 
provide drivers with alerts corresponding to their impor­
tance (risk) levels, focusing more on risky targets closely 
related to safety (Chen et al., 2019) to ensure that informa­
tion is presented within the bounds of available attention 
resources (Herbert et al., 2017; Pankok & Kaber, 2018). We 
posit that a warning prioritization design, predicated on tar­
get risk levels, can optimize the utilization of world-fixed 
graphics in AR-HUDs. Such a design can enhance drivers’ 
focus on critical driving information, particularly during 
emergencies, by facilitating rapid identification of risky tar­
gets and thereby mitigating the visual attention competition 
induced by multiple targets. Concurrently, discerning critical 
environmental cues is imperative for achieving a high level 
of situational awareness (Endsley & Endsley, 1995). From a 
design standpoint, the rational interpretation of presented 
elements to clarify what requires the operator’s attention 
and understanding is fundamental to enhancing situational 
awareness (Endsley & Endsley, 1995). In this context, a 
warning priority design that categorizes the importance of 
warning graphics can aid drivers in comprehending the risk 
levels among various warnings, preventing confusion over 
ambiguous importance levels, and fostering situational 
awareness, which is vital in the complex and dynamic driv­
ing environment.
Furthermore, concerning the aspect of the implementation 
of warning priority, the ITS has advocated that high-priority 
warnings can be distinguished from others by altering visual 
characteristics. For example, This recommendation is sup­
ported by conventional multi-target tracking studies, which 
have found that a significant difference in the description 
between the critical target and non-critical targets leads to 
efficient search (Herbert et al., 2017), and that stimuli with 
high visual saliency are prioritized for attentional selection in 
cluttered scenes (Herbert et al., 2017; Jung et al., 2020). It has 
been reported that visual separation between critical targets 
and other elements, facilitated by surface features or semantic 
information, can enhance tracking performance (Kamkar 
et al., 2020; Wei et al., 2016). As such, color could be an 
effective means as it can differentiate visual saliency and con­
vey different semantic meanings. Some researchers have sug­
gested that the most critical target could be presented in a 
salient color to reduce the detrimental effects of clutter, as 
color is a crucial factor in determining visual search efficiency 
(Beck et al., 2010; Breslow et al., 2009; Rosenholtz et al., 
2007). It is logical to use different colors to represent multiple 
targets based on their importance (risk level) to the driving 
task. The color contrast would aid drivers in identifying and 
quickly focusing on the risky target among multiple AR 
warnings.
However, AR-HUD systems may encounter hue-shifting 
issues due to ambient brightness, background color, and 
other factors (Gabbard et al., 2022; Merenda et al., 2016). 
The dynamic nature of AR graphics might also impact the 
effectiveness of detecting warnings based on color differenti­
ation. Therefore, while previous researchers have attempted 
to use different colored AR bounding boxes for cueing dif­
ferent types of targets (Muela et al., 2021), the effectiveness 
of warning priority, differentiated by color, on visual alloca­
tion still requires validation.
1.3. Motivation
In summary, as previous research on AR-HUD has predom­
inantly focused on single-target augmentation scenarios, this 
study endeavors to explore the significance of multi-target 
augmentation and assess the efficacy of prioritization warn­
ings differentiated by color within this context. Especially

for novice drivers, who frequently face difficulties in effect­
ively scanning the forward area (Underwood et al., 2003), 
tend to focus their attention within a limited scope 
(Chapman et al., 2002), and are inclined to exhibit error- 
prone search behaviors (Twisk, 1995), there is an urgent 
need for real-time multi-target augmentation. However, their 
constrained attentional resources (Kerruish et al., 2022), 
inexperience (Xiang et al., 2024), and incapacity to accur­
ately evaluate potential risks (Twisk, 1995) render them 
more vulnerable to the impact of multiple AR warnings. 
Therefore, this study also aims to provide valuable insights 
from the perspective of novice drivers for the enhancement 
of future AR-HUD interface designs.
Additionally, while most existing research has concentrated 
on behavioral metrics, there is a dearth of studies examining 
the influence of AR-HUD interfaces on eye movement meas­
ures. This study aims to delve into visual attention using a 
broader range of eye-movement metrics, encompassing time 
to first fixation, saccade count, and gaze entropy (both sta­
tionary gaze entropy and gaze transition entropy). These 
entropy metrics provide direct assessments of the overall dis­
tribution of eye movements and the predictability of spatial 
transitions (Krejtz et al., 2015), thereby reflecting the com­
plexity of eye movements (Schieber & Gilland, 2008) and the 
visual search strategies employed by participants (van 
Maarseveen et al., 2018). By integrating gaze entropy with 
conventional eye-tracking metrics, this study aims to augment 
the findings of previous eye movement studies and enable a 
quantitative analysis of variations in eye movement patterns 
across different warning modalities.
2. Method
2.1. Participants
Due to the limitations in G Power’s ability to handle mixed- 
design analyses, an a priori power analysis was conducted 
using MorePower 6 (Campbell & Thompson, 2012). The 
analysis was set with a significance level of 0.05, a power of 
0.8, and an effect size of 0.2. The findings suggested that a 
total sample size exceeding 42 is necessary to achieve the 
desired statistical power. Consequently, this study included 
45 participants (23 males and 22 females), all of whom were 
students from Zhejiang Sci-Tech University in Hangzhou, 
China. The average age of the participants was 22.7 years 
(SD ¼ 2.76). All participants reported having normal vision 
or vision that was corrected to normal, and they all pos­
sessed a valid Chinese driver’s license. The average driving 
experience was 1065 kilometers and 2.7 years, respectively. 
Written informed consent was obtained from each partici­
pant, following the approved research protocol of the 
Institutional Review Board at the Department of Psychology, 
Zhejiang Sci-Tech University. Upon completion of the 
experiment, all participants were compensated.
2.2. Experimental equipment
The experimental environment, as referred to Wang et al. 
(2023), comprised a 72-inch high-definition liquid crystal 
display (LCD) screen (Huayin Vision Technology Co., CN), a 
desktop eye-tracking device (SMI iView Red-M, SensoMotoric 
Instruments, Berlin, Germany) and a chin support. This 
monitor, featuring a resolution of 1920 by 1080 pixels, served 
as the medium for presenting driving video clips. The eye- 
tracking device was capable of calibration at nine points, 
ensuring recordings had a spatial error of less than 0.8 deg in 
both x and y directions. It operates at a sampling rate of 
120 Hz with a latency of less than 20 milliseconds. The sys­
tematic error is 0.5 and the spatial resolution is 0.1. The 
screen was positioned at a vertical distance of 1.7 meters from 
the participants, equating to a visual angle of 41.64 by 24.15 
degrees. The eye tracker was situated on a table, 65 centi­
meters distant from the participants. A chin support was used 
to stabilize participants’ heads to eliminate movement (Wang 
et al., 2016). Furthermore, a keyboard linked to the host com­
puter facilitated the collection of behavioral response times. 
The configuration of the experimental setup is depicted in 
Figure 1. In addition, the scenarios depicted in the video clips 
were constructed using a driving simulator, which incorpo­
rated driving simulator software (STISIMDRIVE M100K, Sys­
tems Technology, Inc., Hawthorne, CA, USA), a steering 
wheel, an accelerator, and a brake pedal (Logitech MOMO, 
Newark, CA, USA). The driving simulation software was used 
to build the driving scenarios, while the steering wheel, accel­
erator, and brake pedals were used to control the ego-vehicle’s 
movement within the scenario when recording videos.
2.3. Materials
Previous research has demonstrated the efficacy of brief 
video clips in assessing situation awareness in driving studies 
(Currano et al., 2021) and in examining drivers’ hazard per­
ception (Crundall et al., 2012). Crundall and Underwood 
(2011) have highlighted the advantage of this method, 
wherein each participant is exposed to identical traffic con­
ditions. To maintain experimental control, we elected to 
emulate prior studies by utilizing video clips as the experi­
mental stimuli. We initially constructed driving scenarios 
using medium-fidelity driving simulation software, which 
facilitated a first-person perspective and a dynamic driving 
environment. Subsequently, an operator maneuvered a 
vehicle through a pre-constructed driving scenario and 
recorded the corresponding driving footage using the soft­
ware. During the drive, the operator controlled the vehicle’s 
movements through the steering wheel, accelerator, brake 
pedal, and other devices within the control system, executing 
operations such as acceleration and deceleration at appropri­
ate moments. This procedure was primarily designed to pro­
vide a more authentic and immersive driving experience. A 
total of 20 videos were developed, each depicting a six-lane 
highway with traffic flowing in both directions. Vehicles, 
pedestrians, traffic lights, and road signs, including direc­
tional and speed limit indicators, were integrated into the 
highway scenes, with all elements appearing at random 
intervals. In each video, the vehicle is shown navigating the 
highway, encountering other vehicles, pedestrians, road 
signs, and traffic lights.

Drawing from the study by Cheng et al. (2023), we incor­
porated two types of risk targets: a leading vehicle that sud­
denly brakes and a pedestrian that suddenly crosses the 
road. To explore the potential impact of varying multi-aug­
mentation conditions (e.g., five or two targets), on the detec­
tion of the risky target, we manipulated the number of 
concurrent targets within the scenario. This was achieved by 
controlling the number of additional targets present simul­
taneously with the risky target. We selected traffic lights, 
road signs, other vehicles, or pedestrians at random to serve 
as these additional targets. We devised four distinct risky 
target event scenarios: (1) a scenario with five targets featur­
ing a braking leading vehicle as the risky target, (2) a scen­
ario with five targets featuring a crossing pedestrian as the 
risky target, (3) a scenario with two targets featuring a brak­
ing leading vehicle as the risky target, and (4) a scenario 
with two targets featuring a crossing pedestrian as the risky 
target. To enhance the realism of the driving scenarios, we 
also included other unpredictable events, such as the vehicle 
speeding or running a red light. Each video randomly fea­
tured 0 to 3 of these unpredictable events, including pedes­
trian collisions, rear-end collisions, speeding, and red-light 
violations, with the timing of these events determined ran­
domly. Following the methodology of Wang et al. (2023), 
each video was set to a duration of 30 seconds.
Subsequently, we introduced different AR graphics into 
the 20 videos to create two distinct AR warning modes: 
Equivalent warning mode and Hierarchical warning mode. 
Equivalent warning modes represent the case of no priori­
tization design, and hierarchical warning modes represent 
the case of prioritization design. Given that both yellow and 
red are appropriate for conveying warnings (Friedrich & 
Vollrath, 2022), and red signifies higher urgency (Friedrich 
& Vollrath, 2022), yellow was designated as the warning 
color for the foundational multi-target augmentation 
(equivalent warning mode), while red or yellow was 
employed based on the target’s risk level in the hierarchical 
warning mode. Both colors were specified in RGB format, 
with red defined as [255, 0, 0] and yellow as [255, 255, 0]. 
Bounding box graphics, known for their effectiveness in AR 
warnings, were utilized in this experiment. In the experi­
ment, we referred to the definition by Wu et al. (2022), cate­
gorizing objects within a certain range of the ego-vehicle as 
medium importance level, while objects within the same 
range that pose collision risks are classified as high impor­
tance level. Since time headway is an important variable in 
the distance-keeping algorithms in driving assistant system 
(Siebert et al., 2014) and the National Road Administration 
of Sweden recommends maintaining a time headway of 
three seconds (Vogel, 2003), we defined a three-second time 
headway on the longitudinal distance as the range threshold 
to trigger the AR warning. To be more specific, we initially 
employed a time headway threshold to trigger AR warnings. 
An AR warning is immediately activated when an object’s 
time headway relative to the ego-vehicle is less than or equal 
to three seconds. In the equivalent warning mode, all objects

2.4. Experimental design
A 3  2 mixed design was manipulated in the present 
experiment. The independent variables comprised the warn­
ing mode (with levels: Equivalent warning, Hierarchical 
warning, and Baseline) as a between-subjects factor, and the 
target number (with levels: five targets and two targets) as a 
within-subjects factor.
The warning mode was operationalized across three dis­
tinct conditions: equivalent warning, hierarchical warning, 
and baseline. In the equivalent warning condition, a uniform 
yellow AR bounding box was employed to alert participants 
to targets in the scene, irrespective of their risk level, pri­
marily to examine the influence of multi-target augmenta­
tion. Conversely, the hierarchical warning condition utilized 
varying colors to distinguish warnings based on the targets’ 
risk levels, primarily to assess the efficacy of warning priori­
tization. The baseline condition was devoid of any AR warn­
ings, serving as a control for comparison.
The target number refers to the total count of targets pre­
sent in the scenario at the time the risky target emerges.

suggest that individuals can track up to four to five identical 
targets simultaneously (Beck et al., 2010; Cavanagh & 
Alvarez, 2005; Hy€on€a et al., 2019), but participants exhibit 
higher rates of perceptual error and higher response latency 
when the number of tracked objects is five (Pylyshyn & 
Storm, 1988). Furthermore, when the tracking targets are 
changed to targets with distinct features in studies of mul­
tiple identity tracking (MIT), it suggests that an individual’s 
ability to track simultaneously may be limited to two differ­
ent targets (Horowitz et al., 2007; Hy€on€a et al., 2019). A 
prior study confirmed that when it comes to tracking the 
features and location of targets, participants can track the 
number of targets of approximately 1.6 items (Hy€on€a et al., 
2019). Given that the main thrust of the study was to 
explore the effects of multiple target augmentation, we com­

in the driving scenario can serve as a key entry point for 
target numbers. It can realistically reflect an individual’s vis­
ual attention when confronted with multiple targets. 
Moreover, a previous study examining the effects of AR 
graphic numbers on vision similarly used two and five AR 
graphic numbers as extreme levels as well (Lee et al., 2024). 
Eventually, in the experiment, the target number in the risky 
driving scenario was set to two levels: two targets and five 
targets, respectively.
The dependent variables encompassed both behavioral 
performance and eye-tracking data. Behavioral performance 
metrics included response rate and reaction time. However, 
the response rate was omitted from the results due to ceiling 
effects observed in subsequent analyses. Reaction time was 
calculated as the interval between the emergence of the risk 
target and the moment participants provided feedback upon 
detecting the risk (Cheng et al., 2023). Eye-tracking data 
analysis focused on time to first fixation (TFF), saccade 
count, and gaze entropy. Gaze entropy, which includes sta­
tionary gaze entropy (Hs) and gaze transition entropy (Ht), 
is an appropriate measure for dynamic eye movement char­
acteristics (Harezlak et al., 2019). The definitions of eye 
movement variables are detailed in Table 1.
2.5. Procedure and tasks
Before their initial visit, informed consent was obtained from 
all participants. Subsequently, participants were assigned to 
different warning modes by the experimenter, who also fami­
liarized them with the warning system and the tasks involved, 
and assisted in calibrating their eye movements. Throughout 
the formal experiment, participants in all three groups were 
directed to view the driving video simulations and to 
immerse themselves in the role of the driver. Participants 
were instructed to promptly press a button upon detecting an 
unpredictable event, such as: (1) the vehicle speeding, (2) the 
vehicle running a red light, (3) a pedestrian crossing the road, 
or (4) the leading vehicle braking. The inclusion of the first 
two events was strategic to mitigate the potential for partici­
pants to react solely based on anticipatory effects (Wang 
et al., 2022).
In total, 24 events were distributed randomly across the 
20 driving video simulations, with the critical risky target 
events—namely, the pedestrian crossing and the leading 
vehicle braking—each occurring four times. During this pro­
cess, the background program recorded participants’ key­
stroke reactions in real time, while the desktop eye-tracking 
device concurrently captured the drivers’ scanning behav­
iors. The participants’ simple push-button responses to risky 
targets served as a metric for their corresponding hazard- 
response abilities (Crundall et al., 2012). All 20 videos were 
programmed to play in a randomized sequence. The sche­
matic diagram of the experimental paradigm is depicted in 
Figure 1(a). Once all videos had concluded, demographic 
information was collected from participants across the three 
groups. The detailed procedure is illustrated in Figure 4.

SMI BeGaze3.2.28. Exclusion criteria led to removing three 
participants from each experimental group due to an eye- 
tracking ratio that fell below the 80% threshold, leaving a 
total of 42 participants (22 males, 20 females) for analysis.

The primary eye movement metrics collected during this 
experiment include time to first fixation, saccade count, and 
gaze entropy. During the eye movement data analysis, to 
obtain the driver’s time to first fixation on the risky target, 
we first sketch the dynamic area of interest (AOI) related to 
the risky target according to the position and size of the AR 
bounding box which cues the risky target in each video. The 
dynamic AOI is synchronized with the AR warning graph­
ics’ dimensions and orientation by adjusting the keyframes 
within the BeGaze software. Considering the three-second 
interval from the risky target event’s onset to the collision in 
the video, the dynamic AOI was configured to persist for 
the same duration. That is, the dynamic AOI is consistent 
with the AR graphics in each video that suggest risky tar­
gets. We then identify the moment the first fixation entered 
the dynamic AOI and calculate the corresponding time to 
the first fixation. The standard settings of a minimum dur­
ation of 80 milliseconds and a maximal dispersion of 100 
pixels were used to define fixations. Besides, a saccade is 
defined as an eye-movement event occurring between the 
latest and preceding blink or fixation. We calculate the sac­
cade count in the time period from the onset of the risky 
target event to the participants’ key press. Lastly, to assess 
the entropy of the eye movement time series, we collected 
participants’ fixation coordinates in the duration from the 
emergence of the risky target to the participants’ key press. 
These fixation coordinates were partitioned into a spatial 
bin of 144 by 90 pixels for the calculation of stationary gaze 
entropy and gaze transition entropy. The calculation formula 
of gaze entropy is referred to the Table 1. This strategy is 
suitable for the formation of state spaces across the visual 
field, leading to the production of representative transition 
distributions (Shiferaw et al., 2018).
3. Result
The experimental data were analyzed utilizing IBM SPSS 
Statistics version 26. A significance level of 0.05 was estab­
lished for all statistical analyses. Initially, we attempted to 
compute the results using a repeated measures analysis of 
variance (ANOVA) with a general linear model (GLM). 
However, this analysis revealed a bias in the residuals, indi­
cating that the dependent variables did not meet the 
assumptions necessary for the application of general linear 
models. Furthermore, given the missing data and the 
repeated measures, treating individual subjects as random 
effects was deemed more appropriate. Consequently, we 
conducted the data analysis using generalized linear mixed 
models (GLMM), with warning modes and target numbers 
as fixed effects, and individual subjects as random effects. 
GLMM integrates the characteristics of linear mixed models, 
which include random effects, and generalized linear mod­
els, which manage non-normal data through the use of link 
functions and exponential families (Bolker et al., 2009; Lo & 
Andrews, 2015). It is recognized as the optimal analytical 
tool for non-normal data involving random effects (Bolker 
et al., 2009). The effects and interactions of each predictor 
were assessed by examining the model effects and estimated 
marginal means. These estimated marginal means were 
employed to evaluate the impact of warning modes across 
various conditions, with the Bonferroni correction applied 
for post-hoc testing.

4. Discussion
In this study, we compare the impact of equivalent warning 
mode and hierarchical warning mode on novice drivers’ 
reaction behavior and eye movements to verify the effective­
ness of the warning priority design under multi-target aug­
mentation conditions. We observe that the hierarchical 
warning mode shortens reaction times and reduces saccade 
counts and gaze entropy in comparison to those in the 
equivalent warning mode, demonstrating the superior per­
formance of the priority design. We further analyze these 
results in the subsequence and summarize the implications 
for future AR-HUD design.
4.1. Hierarchical warning in multi-target augmentation
Firstly, our analysis of reaction times indicates that across 
varying target numbers, the equivalent warning mode

consistently exhibits the longest reaction times, while the 
hierarchical warning mode consistently alleviates this nega­

scenarios. The reaction time to a critical target is essential 
for driving safety (Mackenzie & Harris, 2015), and thus, this

gration of warning priority design with multi-target scen­
arios, as a necessary and effective approach to mitigate the 
extended reaction times associated with multi-target aug­

Upon further examination of the time to first fixation, we 
encountered an unexpected result. The hierarchical warning 
mode achieves the shortest time to first fixation under a 
two-target condition, yet no significant difference is 
observed in the first fixation time between the hierarchical 
warning mode and the equivalent warning mode, under a 
five-target condition. The time to first fixation is a critical 
measure of a driver’s risk perception capability (Cheng 
et al., 2023). Consequently, the hierarchical warning mode, 
despite its utility, appears to have limited effectiveness in 
enhancing risk perception in multi-target scenarios, espe­
cially with an increased number of targets. We believe that 
this result once again emphasizes the strong impact of atten­
tion dispersion under multi-target augmentation, causing 
participants’ attention to be divided among several targets, 
which leads to an extended time to achieve the first fixation 
even under warning priority design conditions. It reminds 
that the color-based warning priority design may not sub­
stantially improve a driver’s risk perception at the percep­
tion stage and may not constitute the most effective warning 
form. This insight diverges from the prevailing notion that 
color prominence is paramount for enhancing visual search 
efficiency (Beck et al., 2010). However, we hypothesize that 
this discrepancy could also be attributed to the influence of 
background colors and ambient lighting on the visibility of 
AR graphics (Gabbard et al., 2022; Merenda et al., 2016), 
which might impair the effectiveness of warning priority in 
the perception stage. In addition, we think that another pos­
sibility is due to our use of bounding boxes for warning 
within the scene, where color differences are mainly 
reflected in the contours of the box graphics, which may, to 
some extent, weaken the effect of color-based warning 
priority.
The results presented elicit a critical question: why does 
the hierarchical warning mode achieve the shortest reaction 
times without concomitantly reducing the time to the first 
fixation? For instance, despite equivalent times to the first 
fixation, the equivalent warning mode is associated with the 
longest reaction times, whereas the hierarchical warning 
mode significantly reduces this duration. In light of these 
findings, we seek to elucidate their implications by integrating 
them with the concept of situational awareness. Situational 
awareness is essential for effective decision-making, particu­
larly in dynamic settings (Endsley & Endsley, 1995). It 
encompasses three levels: detecting environmental elements 
(perception, level 1), comprehending their significance 
(understanding, level 2), and anticipating future developments 
(prediction, level 3). Consequently, the progression from the 
emergence of a risky target to participant response can be 
divided into two stages (Cheng et al., 2023; Mackenzie & 
Harris, 2015), including a perception stage, which involves 
identifying targets and corresponds to level 1 of situational 
awareness, and a verification stage, which involves assessing 
risks and is associated with levels 2 and 3 of situational 
awareness. We posit that warning modes differentially affect 
the verification stage, thereby influencing the development of 
levels 2 and 3 of situational awareness in drivers. Notably, 
situational awareness necessitates the integration of disparate 
elements. For instance, level 2 transcends mere awareness of 
present elements to include an appreciation of their signifi­
cance (Endsley & Endsley, 1995; Teichmann & Motus, 2021). 
Thus, it is the synthesis of detailed situational information 
and how this information is assembled for understanding and 
projection that ultimately shapes decision-making (Endsley & 
Endsley, 1995). During the experiment, the equivalent warn­
ing mode’s prolonged reaction times are likely attributable to 
its absence of a warning priority design, employing similar 
yellow AR graphics that may obscure the distinction between 
risky and non-risky targets. This similarity can not only 
impede the identification of critical objects (Chicherov & 
Herzog, 2015), but also impair drivers’ comprehension of the 
meaning of the AR graphics. The ambiguity associated with 
this equivalent augmentation is a particular challenge for nov­
ice drivers since they are not good at recognizing potential 
hazards (Underwood et al., 2003). Consequently, even after 
fixating on the risky target, drivers must conduct additional 
scans to verify the risk levels of other targets, and confirm 
the meaning of these warning cues, prolonging the reaction 
time. In contrast, the hierarchical warning mode, incorporat­
ing a color-based warning priority design, conveys distinct 
semantic meanings through the AR graphics’ colors, delineat­
ing the boundaries between the risky target and other targets. 
This distinction reduces the similarity between risky and 
remaining targets, reduces unnecessary scanning of non-risky 
targets, simplifies the validation process, promotes the pro­
gression of situational awareness, and leads to the shortest 
response time. It is especially beneficial for novice drivers. 
Overall, the extent to which displays provide information that 
is processed and integrated positively impacts situational 
awareness, which in turn influences the final decision 
(Endsley & Endsley, 1995). This finding confirms that the 
benefits of hierarchical warning modes extend beyond aiding 
in the perception of critical warnings; more importantly, they 
reduce the cognitive load associated with the ambiguity that 
arises from the concurrent presentation of AR graphics. 
Considering the varying importance of objects within the 
driving environment, we posit that the warning classification 
provided by the priority design is particularly indispensable 
under conditions characterized by multi-target augmentation.
The analysis of saccadic eye movements also supports the 
potential efficacy of hierarchical warning systems in mitigat­
ing superfluous scanning behaviors and promoting a higher 
level of situational awareness. For example, the equivalent 
warning mode, devoid of integrated warning priority, is 
observed to produce the highest saccade counts. In contrast, 
the hierarchical warning mode, which incorporates the

warning priority design, demonstrates a significant reduction

Furthermore, integrating gaze entropy measurements into 
our study provides a fresh vantage point for the enhanced 
comprehension of the influence of warning modes. Gaze 
entropy serves as a metric for assessing visual scanning 
behaviors during tasks with substantial visuospatial complex­
ity, such as driving (Shiferaw et al., 2018). It encapsulates 
the intricacy of visual scanning as dictated by the statistical 
distribution of elementary visual features or the information 
density within the visual field (Shiferaw et al., 2019). 
Notably, stationary gaze entropy measures the overall spatial 
dispersion of gaze, whereas gaze transition entropy captures 
the complexity of gaze shift patterns, indicative of search 
efficiency (Shiferaw et al., 2019). Higher values of stationary 
gaze entropy denote a more extensive dispersion of gaze 
(Shiferaw et al., 2018), whereas elevated gaze transition 
entropy suggests increased randomness in eye movements 
and a higher frequency of gaze shifts among interest areas 
(Ebeid & Gwizdka, 2018). Consequently, this experiment 
employs gaze entropy to ascertain the efficacy of warning 
modes in modulating driver scanning behavior within haz­
ardous driving contexts from a quantitative standpoint. 
Consistent with expectations, the equivalent warning mode 
is associated with markedly higher levels of both stationary 
and gaze transition entropy, signifying a wider gaze span 
and more frequent shifts in attention, indicative of greater 
visual disorder. Conversely, the hierarchical warning mode 
is characterized by the lowest levels of these entropy meas­
ures, implying a more focused gaze and reduced attentional 
shifts, thereby robustly evidencing the efficacy of warning 
priority design in curtailing superfluous scanning behaviors 
among drivers. This result confirms our suspicion to some 
extent.
In summary, the findings validate the hypothesis that 
integrating a color-based warning priority system with 
multi-target augmentation—referred to as the hierarchical 
warning mode—effectively mitigates the adverse effects typ­
ically associated with multi-target scenarios. This is demon­
strated by a significant decrease in saccade count, stationary 
gaze entropy, and gaze transition entropy. Specifically, the 
efficacy of the color-based warning priority design is likely 
attributed to its capacity to reduce unnecessary scanning 
activities during the verification stage, which pertains to lev­
els 2 and 3 of situational awareness. This design demon­
strates effectiveness in minimizing additional cognitive 
demands stemming from the ambiguity of multiple targets 
and in facilitating the driver’s comprehension of multiple 
warnings in scenarios involving multi-target augmentation.
4.2. Implications for AR-HUD design
Prior research on Augmented Reality Head-Up Display (AR- 
HUD) interface design has demonstrated that AR integra­
tion can facilitate driver detection of hazardous targets. This 
study advances the supportive role of AR to scenarios 
involving multiple target augmentations, offering innovative 
insights for the evolution of AR-HUD design. Firstly, our 
research corroborates the notion that employing similar AR 
graphics without an integrated warning priority can degrade 
a driver’s ability to discern risky targets. Concurrently, the 
incorporation of warning priority into interface design 
emerges as an efficacious strategy to neutralize this adverse 
effect, enabling drivers to discern risky targets among many, 
expedite the verification process, and promote a higher level 
of situational awareness. This underscores the criticality of 
integrating warning priority design considerations for AR 
graphics in AR-HUD multi-target augmentation interfaces. 
Furthermore, the research analysis posits that the impact of 
AR graphics is predominantly evident during the verification 
stage subsequent to perception. This finding broadens our 
comprehension of AR-HUD graphic effects and highlights 
the necessity for interface designs to account for graphic 
influences on the driver’s verification stage to reduce cogni­
tive interference and augment system efficacy and safety. 
Ultimately, while color-based warning priority design enhan­
ces driver perception of risk under conditions of limited tar­
gets, its utility is constrained in scenarios with an 
abundance of targets. Hence, future AR-HUD warning pri­
ority designs must consider alternative formats for optimiz­
ing both perception and verification stages and contemplate 
the optimal quantity of AR-augmented targets presentations.
5. Limitations
We recognize that the findings of this study are subject to 
limitations regarding their generalizability and evidential 
force. Firstly, this study, akin to previous research (Chen 
et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2022), utilized video clips as stimuli 
with the aim of investigating the impact of multi-target aug­
mentation on drivers’ visual perception while controlling for 
extraneous variables through video experiments. Despite 
efforts to replicate real driving conditions, the interactive 
complexities of actual driving may exceed those observed in 
the video (Mackenzie & Harris, 2015), potentially undermin­
ing the ecological validity of our findings and their real-world 
applicability. Future research should integrate simulated driv­
ing to assess the impact of multi-target augmentation on driv­
ing performance. Second, the study’s focus on novice drivers 
offers insights into AR-HUD interface design tailored to this 
demographic. However, experienced drivers possess more 
refined visual attention allocation and scene comprehension, 
suggesting their performance with AR-HUD multi-target aug­
mentation may differ significantly. Therefore, the study’s 
emphasis on novice drivers limits the generalizability of the 
results, and future studies should include experienced drivers 
for a more comprehensive AR-HUD interface design. Third, 
as noted in the introduction, real AR-HUD use involves

6. Conclusion
This research explores the effects of the AR-HUD on driver 
behavior within a multi-target environment, with a focus on 
reaction times and eye movement patterns in response to risky 
targets. Our goal was to evaluate the efficacy of warning prior­
ity design within multi-target scenarios. By comparing the out­
comes of the equivalent warning mode, hierarchical warning 
mode, and a baseline group, we have advanced our under­
standing of the impact of multi-target conditions and the 
effectiveness of warning priority design within this context. 
The hierarchical warning mode notably exhibited superior per­
formance, characterized by the shortest reaction times, minimal 
saccade count, and reduced gaze entropy across different target 
number conditions, thereby enhancing the efficiency of driver 
responses and the precision of visual scanning. Our analysis 
suggests that the hierarchical warning mode’s effectiveness may 
stem from its optimization of the situational awareness in the 
verification stage. The results, encompassing reaction time, eye 
movement metrics, and gaze entropy, collectively endorse the 
utility of warning prioritization in AR-HUDs under conditions 
of multi-target augmentation. These insights not only broaden 
our comprehension of AR graphics’ role in multi-target scen­
arios but also offer guidance for the future evolution of AR- 
HUD technology. It is important to note that the time to first 
fixation did not significantly vary with an increasing number 
of targets in the hierarchical warning mode relative to other 
modes. This finding implies that the color-based priority warn­
ing system’s effectiveness may diminish under conditions with 
a higher target density. Consequently, this insight encourages 
further reflection on determining the optimal quantity of AR 
graphics and the most effective presentation methods for prior­
ity information.
