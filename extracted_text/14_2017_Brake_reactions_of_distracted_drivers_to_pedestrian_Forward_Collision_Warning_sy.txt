Introduction: Forward Collision Warning (FCW) can be effective in directing driver attention towards a conﬂict
20
and thereby aid in preventing or mitigating collisions. FCW systems aiming at pedestrian protection have been
21
introduced onto the market, yet an assessment of their safety beneﬁts depends on the accurate modeling of
22
driver reactions when the system is activated. This study contributes by quantifying brake reaction time and
23
brake behavior (deceleration levels and jerk) to compare the effectiveness of an audio-visual warning only, an
24
added haptic brake pulse warning, and an added Head-Up Display in reducing the frequency of collisions with
25
pedestrians. Further, this study provides a detailed data set suited for the design of assessment methods for
26
car-to-pedestrian FCW systems. Method: Brake response characteristics were measured for heavily distracted
27
drivers who were subjected to a single FCW event in a high-ﬁdelity driving simulator. The drivers maintained
28
a self-regulated speed of 30 km/h in an urban area, with gaze direction diverted from the forward roadway by
29
a secondary task. Results: Collision rates and brake reaction times differed signiﬁcantly across FCW settings.
30
Brake pulse warnings resulted in the lowest number of collisions and the shortest brake reaction times (mean
31
0.8 s, SD 0.29 s). Brake jerk and deceleration were independent of warning type. Ninety percent of drivers exceeded
32
a maximum deceleration of 3.6 m/s2 and a jerk of 5.3 m/s3. Conclusions: Brake pulse warning was the most effective
33
FCW interface for preventing collisions. In addition, this study presents the data required for driver modeling for
34
car-to-pedestrian FCW similar to Euro NCAP's 2015 car-to-car FCW assessment. Practical applications: Vehicle
35
manufacturers should consider the introduction of brake pulse warnings to their FCW systems. Euro NCAP could
36
introduce an assessment that quantiﬁes the safety beneﬁts of pedestrian FCW systems and thereby aid the prolif-
37
eration of effective systems.

1. Introduction
50
Driver distraction is a major factor in vehicle-to-pedestrian collisions.
51
Approximately one third of fatal pedestrian accidents in Japan are caused
52
by distracted drivers (ITARDA, 2012), a conclusion further supported by
53
recent in-depth analyses of Japanese naturalistic driving data that found
54
that the primary cause of vehicle-to-pedestrian collisions is the diversion
55
of drivers' attention to something other than the pedestrian at risk
56
(Habibovic, Tivesten, Uchida, Bärgman, & Ljung Aust, 2013).
57
In situations of potential conﬂict, Forward Collision Warning (FCW)
58
systems may be effective in directing driver attention towards the
59
conﬂict and thereby help prevent or mitigate a collision. Automated
60
Emergency Braking (AEB) may also prevent or mitigate collisions by
61
braking the car automatically without driver input in certain conditions.
62
FCW and AEB systems designed with the aim of protecting pedestrians
63
have recently been introduced on the car market (Lubbe & Davidsson,
64
2015).
65
FCW systems make use of auditory, visual, and haptic Human
66
Machine Interfaces (HMIs) and must balance driver acceptance with
67
effectiveness in terms of allowing time for a driver to brake the vehicle.
68
The earlier and more intrusive the warning, the more effectively a
69
system can prevent collisions. But at the same time, driver acceptance
70
of such systems decreases, and it has been suggested that drivers are
71
then more likely to ignore the system, turn it off, or refrain from having
72
such systems installed
Q3
(Lubbe & Davidsson, 2015). This claim is empir-
73
ically supported by an observational study of car owners' active safety
74
settings: Lane Departure Warning, which was more annoying, was
75
found to be switched off more often than FCW, which was less annoying
76
(IIHS, 2016). A FCW system might therefore act as an Imminent Crash
77
Warning with a rather late activation, aiming for immediate driver
78
reaction, or as a Cautionary Crash Warning with early activation, aiming
79
at directing attention and raising risk awareness, or may incorporate
80
both functions in a triggered approach (Campbell, Richard, Brown, &
81
McCallum, 2007; Naujoks, Grattenthaler, & Neukum, 2012).

82
Assessment methods for evaluating the safety beneﬁt of pedestrian
83
active safety systems including AEB and FCW are under development
84
by Euro NCAP (Euro NCAP, 2015a, 2015b) and NHTSA (Yanagisawa,
85
Swanson, & Najm, 2014). Much attention has been focused on AEB;
86
the automated nature of these systems requires no driver input and
87
simpliﬁes the development of test and evaluation procedures. The
88
Euro NCAP pedestrian active safety assessment, introduced in 2016,
89
includes AEB and FCW evaluation. Euro NCAP has a part score related
90
to AEB effectiveness and a part score related to HMI evaluation. AEB is
91
scored for its ability to reduce vehicle speed prior to a simulated pedes-
92
trian impact in four different test scenarios at driving speeds gradually
93
increasing from 20 to 60 km/h. A maximum of 18 scoring points are
94
assigned to each scenario, depending on the speed reduction resulting
95
from testing. FCW is not tested for its ability to reduce impact speed,
96
but is part of the HMI evaluation. FCW is assigned a score of 1 point if
97
operating above 40 km/h and warning at a Time-To-Collision (TTC) of
98
at least 1.2 s (Euro NCAP, 2015b). Further components of the HMI eval-
99
uation are an assessment of the system's ease of deactivation (2 points)
100
and night-time operation (1 point). The overall active safety system
101
score is weighted 5:1 for AEB to HMI. As the FCW activation accounts
102
for one fourth of the HMI score, the balance between AEB and FCW is
103
20:1. Clearly, the relevance of FCW compared to AEB is low in Euro
104
NCAP's assessment. However, the extent to which this reﬂects the actual
105
safety beneﬁts of these systems has yet to be analyzed.
106
Driver models, in particular assumptions of driver reaction times
107
and response inputs, are essential in assessing the ability of FCW sys-
108
tems to reduce collisions (Brown, Lee, & McGehee, 2001; McLaughlin,
109
Hankey, & Dingus, 2008). Brake response is more relevant to FCW
110
beneﬁt assessment than steer response as drivers are more likely to
111
respond by braking even in situations where steering would have
112
been more effective in avoiding collision (Adams, 1994). Crucial to the
113
effectiveness of a given FCW system is driver brake reaction time
114
(Brown et al., 2001), brake deceleration and brake jerk. Simply, the ear-
115
lier and harder the brakes are applied, the higher the speed reduction.
116
For the Euro NCAP safety beneﬁt assessment of car-to-car FCW systems,
117
brakes are applied by a driving robot with brake characteristics speciﬁed
118
by the car manufacturer to achieve a deceleration of 4 m/s2 within 0.2 to
119
0.4 s (Euro NCAP, 2015c). The speciﬁcation of these characteristics
120
for pedestrian FCW systems needs to reﬂect true driver behavior. Such
121
behavior has attracted little attention thus far.
122
1.1. Brake response for car-to-car FCW
123
Braking reaction times to FCW have been studied for a variety of
124
FCW systems in simulated car-to-car rear-end collisions (overviews
125
for example in Campbell et al., 2007; Green et al., 2008; Mayer et al.,
126
2011). When warned, drivers must take notice of the warning, decode
127
and understand it, and respond (Ho & Spence, 2008).
128
Most FCW systems on the market use a combination of visual and
129
auditory warnings (ADAC, 2011). Almost all existing FCW systems in
130
passenger cars use visual displays in the instrument cluster (i.e. near
131
the speedometer) while a few use head-up displays (HUD) projecting
132
the warning signal onto the windscreen area (Wege, Will, & Victor,
133
2013). Visual displays can readily indicate distinctive threats using
134
icons, but may not be noticed if the driver's view is diverted (Ho,
135
Spence, & Tan, 2005). Head-Up Displays (HUD) can be located closer
136
to the natural gaze direction of the driver to the forward roadway and
137
may be less likely to divert driver attention from the trafﬁc scene com-
138
pared to displays in the instrument cluster, but it remains unclear
139
whether HUD improves driver reaction compared to instrument cluster
140
warnings (Wege et al., 2013). One aim of this study is to quantify the
141
potential beneﬁts of an added HUD to an audio-visual warning for
142
pedestrian encounters.
143
Haptic warnings have only been recently introduced as warning
144
interfaces; studies show a potential for improved driver reactions (Ho &
145
Spence, 2008). Some haptic warnings are always noticeable (e.g. seat
146
vibration, or brake pulse) and were shown to be effective in car-to-car
147
rear-end collision driving simulator experiments (Chun, Han, Park, Seo,
148
& Choi, 2012; Mohebbi, Gray, & Tan, 2009; Scott & Gray, 2008). Other
149
haptic warnings depend on the driver being in contact with the warning
150
interface (e.g. pedal vibration), which reduces their overall effectiveness
151
(Campbell et al., 2007).
152
The ﬁndings for the effectiveness of a short brake pulse, as one spe-
153
ciﬁc type of haptic warning, are inconclusive. In a car-to-car rear-end
154
collision driving simulator experiment, signiﬁcantly lower absolute
155
maximum brake deceleration for a brake pulse warning compared to
156
an audio-visual warning was reported. Further, both audio-visual and
157
brake pulse warnings were reported to elicit faster brake reaction than
158
no warning at all by trend (approximately 0.4 s reductions), but the
159
difference was not signiﬁcant. (Lerner et al., 2011).
160
Brown et al. (2005) reported a reduced number of potential collisions
161
due to driver braking for brake pulse warnings compared to no warnings
162
in an intersection car-to-car collision warning test track study; however,
163
brake reaction times are not signiﬁcantly shorter for the brake pulse
164
warning.
165
Kiefer et al. (1999) reported longer reaction times for brake pulse
166
warnings combined with other warning modalities. Brake pulse–visual
167
warnings lead to longer reaction times than audio-visual warnings
168
alone in a car-to-car forward collision warning track study. In a driving
169
simulator experiment of car-to-car rear-end collisions, Ljung Aust
170
(2014) reported belt–pretensioning–audio and brake pulse–audio warn-
171
ings having similar effectiveness in signiﬁcantly reducing reaction times
172
by approximately 0.3 s compared to an audio-visual warning. In a test
173
track experiment of car-to-car rear-end collisions, Kolke, Gauss, and
174
Silvestro (2012) reported reaction times shortened by one-third
175
(approximately 0.3 s, non-signiﬁcant) when a brake pulse was added
176
to an audio-visual warning.
177
No studies can be found that measured brake reaction times for brake
178
pulses warnings, separate or when combined with other warnings, in
179
car-to-pedestrian conﬂicts. Another aim of this study, therefore, is to
180
quantify potential beneﬁts of adding a brake pulse to an audio-visual
181
warning for pedestrian encounters.
182
1.2. Brake response for pedestrian FCW
183
Pedestrians are different from cars and the perceived criticality of
184
and driver reactions to car-to-car FCW and pedestrian FCW are not nec-
185
essarily the same. Pedestrians often enter the driver's ﬁeld of view from
186
the side (Wisch, Pastor, Zander, & Lorenz, 2012; Yanagisawa et al.,
187
2014), and are a threat smaller in size than a car-to-car collision threat.
188
Event criticality has been shown to inﬂuence driver reaction to FCW
189
(Ljung Aust, Engström, & Viström, 2013). Thus, one cannot necessarily
190
assume that the time needed to decode the warning and the driver re-
191
sponse is equal for car-to-car and pedestrian threats. Maag, Schneider,
192
Lübbeke, Weisswange, and Goerick (2015) studied drivers approaching
193
vehicle and non-vehicle (including pedestrian) threats and found that
194
driver response is stronger for vehicle threats. It seems thus necessary
195
to study driver behavior in pedestrian encounters in order to obtain a
196
driver model for pedestrian FCW assessment.
197
The differences observed between warning timings in car-to-car
198
FCW and car-to-pedestrian FCW provides another motivation to study
199
pedestrian FCW. For car-to-car FCW studies, typical ranges are from 2
200
to 5 s TTC (Jenkins, Stanton, Walker, & Yong, 2007); for example, TTC
201
is 5 s in Mohebbi et al. (2009), 4 s in Chun et al. (2012) and both 3
202
and 5 s in Scott and Gray (2008). Car-to-pedestrian FCW was measured
203
to activate at a TTC of 1.8 s in a production vehicle (Matsui, Han, &
204
Mizuno, 2011), and thus appears to warn later. Warning time is known
205
to inﬂuence driver reaction (e.g. Hirst & Graham, 1997; Scott & Gray,
206
2008).
207
Studies of driver reaction times to pedestrian collisions in simulated
208
vehicles are sparse. Straughn, Gray, and Tan (2009) reported steering
209
reaction times for pedestrian FCW. For a warning given at 4 s TTC, haptic

1.3. Objectives
227
This study aims to quantify brake reaction time and brake behavior
228
(deceleration levels and jerk) to provide a detailed data set suited for
229
the design of safety beneﬁt assessment methods for pedestrian FCW
230
systems. Of particular interest to this study were three HMIs: an
231
audio-visual warning, a combination of brake pulse and audio-visual
232
warning, and a novel HUD design highlighting the threat in combination
233
with an audio-visual warning.
234
The hypotheses for this study were as follows.
235
• Among FCW systems, Brake pulse and a novel HUD show higher
236
reductions than an audio-visual only HMI.
237
• Brake reaction time, brake deceleration, and brake jerk are dependent
238
on HMI. Among FCW systems, Brake pulse and novel HUD show shorter
239
reaction times as compared to audio-visual only. Familiarization with
240
the HUD HMI reduces reaction times compared to naive drivers.
241
• Familiarization with the HUD HMI reduces collision rates and reaction
242
times compared to naive drivers with the same HMI.
243
244
Besides testing the hypotheses, driver brake reaction times, brake
245
deceleration and brake jerk, as well as their correlation, are collected
246
and reported here to provide a complete dataset for future modeling
247
of driver reactions to pedestrian FCWs in safety beneﬁt assessments.
248
2. Methods
249
Volunteers drove in a simulated urban environment in a moving-
250
base driving simulator. In a single surprise event, when drivers were
251
distracted by a secondary task on a screen mounted on the passenger
252
seat, a simulated pedestrian crossed the road in front of the vehicle on
253
a collision course with the vehicle. Drivers were warned of the imminent
254
threat using different FCW systems. Differences in driver reaction to the
255
FCW were analyzed in a between-subject experimental design.
256
2.1. Driving simulator
257
Experiments were conducted in the Toyota driving simulator located
258
at the Toyota Motor Corporation Higashifuji Technical Center in Japan
259
(Fig. 1). The simulator uses an actual vehicle placed on a platform
260
housed within a dome with a diameter of 7.1 m. A 360-degree view
261
is projected on the inside wall of the dome, which is mounted on a
262
6-degrees-of-freedom motion base. The motion base can move horizon-
263
tally within a 35 × 20 m range (Adachi et al., 2014).
264
Volunteers signed a consent form and were then instructed to main-
265
tain a speed of 30 km/h and react to trafﬁc as usual before completing a

301
The experiments were conducted with three different HMIs. The
302

303

304

305

306
- Setting 1 “AV” replicated a Toyota Prius model year 2013 FCW HMI
307

308

309
consisted of a ﬂashing screen in the instrument cluster near the
310

311

312
(Fig. 3, left).
313
- Setting 2 “BP” added a short brake pulse, also activated at TTC 1.8 s, to
314
Setting 1. The triangular brake pulse had a duration of approximately
315

316
systems of the Lexus LS model year 2014. This brake pulse was inten-
317

318
- Setting 3 “HUD” issued the same warning as Setting 1 at TTC 1.8 s in a
319

320

321

322

2.3. Volunteer characteristics
338
Fifty-eight volunteers approximately representing the gender and
339
age distribution of Japanese driving license holders (Statistics Bureau,
340
2016) were included in the analysis, matched across the four settings
341
with mean and standard deviation values as presented in Table 2.
342
Gender, age (range: 19 to 78 years) and annual driving distance
343
(range: 10 to 35,000 km) were self-reported and did not differ signiﬁ-
344
cantly between groups (age: ANOVA, df = 48, F = 0,73, p = 0.54;
345

346
exact test, p = 0.96).
347
Volunteers were recruited sequentially to obtain 6 controls and 13
348
valid tests for each of the four settings, totaling 58 valid tests. In order
349
to obtain these numbers, a total of 101 volunteers were recruited. Of
350
these, 43 were excluded for the following reasons: Tests were incom-
351
plete and no data were available due to motion sickness (6 cases);
352
volunteers reported not driving normally during the test (5 cases); volun-
353
teers applied the brakes prior to HMI activation (27 cases); and volun-
354
teers returned the gaze to the forward roadway during the secondary
355
task (5 cases). The control group was used to verify the distraction task;
356
results are not used in analyses due to its small size.
357
It should be noted that applying the brake prior to HMI activation
358
does not indicate a failure to engage volunteers in the secondary task
359
for two reasons: First, most volunteers who activated the brake prior
360
to HMI activation in the experiment also did so in the preceding ﬁve
361
training situations. Brake application seemed to be triggered by the
362
onset of the secondary task and the volunteers' desire to reduce speed
363
when diverting the gaze from the forward roadway. Second, brake
364
deceleration levels were insufﬁcient to avoid collisions, indicating that
365
these volunteers did not spot the pedestrian and were not trying to
366
avoid a collision prior to HMI activation.

2.5. Data analysis
380
For each setting, the number of collisions and the number of colli-
381

382

383

384
number of brake responses prior to the collision event was compared
385
between settings.
386
Brake reaction times were measured from the time of warning issue
387
at 1.8 s TTC, common for all settings, until brake initiation, deﬁned as a
388
brake pedal force exceeding 10 N. Differences in settings were analyzed
389
using one-way ANOVA and the Multiple Comparisons function (Tukey's
390
honestly signiﬁcant difference criterion) in the MATLAB R2013a statisti-
391
cal toolbox. All statistical tests were carried out at a signiﬁcance level of
392
5%. All boxplots depict median, ﬁrst and third quartile; maximum whis-
393
ker length is 1.5 times the difference between ﬁrst and third quartile.
394
Data points beyond the whiskers are deﬁned and depicted as outliers
395
using a red + symbol.

be assigned to the FCW with complete certainty; drivers might not have
398

399

4. Discussion
502
In this study, three different forward collision warning HMIs were
503
tested on independent groups of 13 subjects in a simulated scenario in
504
which a pedestrian crossed the road while the driver's attention was
505
distracted from the forward roadway. The effectiveness of an audio-
506
visual warning only, an added haptic brake pulse warning, and an
507
added Head-Up Display indicating the nature and position of the threat
508
in reducing the frequency of collisions with pedestrians, was studied. A
509
fourth setting comprised the HUD plus an additional familiarization
510
procedure conducted before the test drive. Results showed that brake
511
pulse warnings resulted in the lowest number of collisions and the
512
shortest brake reaction times (mean 0.8 s, SD 0.29 s). Brake jerk and de-
513
celeration were independent of warning type. Ninety percent of drivers
514
exceed a maximum deceleration of 3.6 m/s2 and a jerk of 5.3 m/s3.
515
Brake behavior was recorded to provide data needed for driver model-
516
ing in safety beneﬁt assessments.
517
The audio-visual and brake pulse warnings in this study were set to
518
activate at a time-to-collision of 1.8 s, matching a pedestrian FCW sys-
519
tem of a production car reported by Matsui et al. (2011). This warning
520
time is in the middle of what Helmer (2014) found to be the optimal
521
range of 1.5 to 2.2 s TTC based on trafﬁc simulation and balancing
522
false activations with injury reduction. Naujoks et al. (2012) found
523
that Cautionary Crash Warnings were appreciated by drivers in a volun-
524
teers test as early as 2 to 3 s prior to a conﬂict evolving into an unavoid-
525
able collision. In line with this ﬁnding, the HUD was activated at 2.5 s
526
TTC, as an earlier cautionary warning.
527
Homma, Kikuchi, Wakasugi, Tasaka, and Yoshida (2014) report that
528
only 73% of drivers acknowledging notice of an audio-visual FCW for an
529
imminent but unexpected car-to-car rear-end collision in a track test
530
abandoned the secondary task. The remainder did not respond to the
531
warning, which was attributed to misjudgment of the urgency and
532
warning content. These results are comparable to a reaction rate of
533
62% of the auditory–visual warnings in Setting 1, which may also
534
include cases where the warning was not noticed at all (Table 3).
535
The HUD in settings 3 and 4 aimed to assist drivers in locating the
536
threat and inferring the type of threat in line with the recommendation
537
from Campbell et al. (2007) that “warnings should induce an orienting
538
response, where appropriate, causing the driver to look in the direction

561

562

563
“sub-optimal characteristics of haptic alerts,” that is using a haptic
564

565

566

568

569

570

571

572

573

574
quicker brake application once the driver was looking forward. This ef-
575

576
lead to the conclusion that the reduction of reaction time, in stemming
577
from the redirection of attention, is independent of the collision object.
578
Thus this study provides some evidence that the brake pulse warning
579
for distracted drivers is not only effective for pedestrian encounters,
580
but also likely to be applicable to other types of imminent collisions.

4.1. Strengths and limitations
582
Driving simulators offer a safe and controlled environment for test-
583
ing driver response and virtual pedestrian models can move more real-
584
istically than in test track studies (Chrysler, Ahmad, & Schwarz, 2015).
585
An advanced moving-base driving simulator is required to realistically
586
provide brake pulse warnings, of which only a few exist. Toyota's
587
high-ﬁdelity moving-base driving simulator was used, which has
588
previously been shown to be able to reproduce driver behavior when
589
encountering pedestrians observed in a test track study (Lubbe &
590
Davidsson, 2015). The use of this particular driving simulator gives
591
strength to the ﬁndings.
592
An advanced driving simulator is, however, costly to run and only a
593
limited number of participants comprise each group. While some
594
interesting ﬁndings reported here reach statistical signiﬁcance, more
595
conclusions could be drawn from a larger sample size.
596
Further, only demographic information was collected from the
597
participants. Personality characteristics, such as sensation seeking,
598
which may inﬂuence the reliability and generalizability of these ﬁnd-
599
ings, are not available.
600
The secondary task proved to be reliable in diverting the drivers'
601
gaze and was simple to verify from video recordings of the drivers.
602
However, the task is not naturalistic, and might give rise to exaggerated
603
distraction levels compared to those encountered in everyday driving.
604
Volunteers were instructed to drive at 30 km/h. This speed might
605
have been above or below what the volunteers would have felt to be ap-
606
propriate in that driving situation. The 27 excluded cases of volunteers
607
applying the brakes before warning (reported in Section 2.3) indicate
608
that, for a considerable proportion of volunteers, the driving speed
609
was perceived as too high for proper engagement with the secondary
610
task, hence brake application. Those volunteers who did not apply the
611
brakes prior to the warning might have been comfortable with the driv-
612
ing speed, an assumption supported by the post-experiment statement
613
of driving normally. However, it is also possible that these volunteers
614
also felt uncomfortable with the driving speed but avoided applying
615
the brakes in order to comply with instructions, and did not relate this
616
behavior to un-normal driving. If this were true, the validity of the

4.2. Application and future research needs
640
Campbell et al. (2007) noted design issues related to haptic warnings
641
and mentions, among other things, kinematic changes to the driver's
642
position and driver misperceptions and annoyance. These issues are rel-
643
evant, and can be major obstacles to introducing such systems to the
644
market. For example, false positive activations of this study's high inten-
645
sity pulse might be perceived as annoying and lead to deactivation of the
646
FCW system. Driver acceptance of brake pulse warnings therefore needs
647
to be studied further.
648
However, a few systems providing a short brake pulse to alert the
649
driver have been introduced to the market for car-to-car FCW (ADAC,
650
2011; Hayashi et al., 2013). These systems have not yet been adopted
651
widely and there are presumably challenges there to be addressed; nev-
652
ertheless, it is encouraging to think that it might be possible to introduce
653
this warning type, shown here to be effective for pedestrian encounters,
654
in the future.
655
Driver models are needed for assessment schemes such as Euro
656
NCAP's pedestrian AEB rating. In car-to-car AEB assessment, driver
657
reaction to an FCW is modeled by a brake reaction time of 1.2 s and a
658
maximum brake level of 4 m/s2 (Euro NCAP, 2015c). Such driver reac-
659
tion for pedestrian FCW might be modeled after data from pedestrian
660
FCW experiments to which this study contributes.
661
First, the proportion of drivers reacting at all and initiating braking
662
should be modeled separately for each FCW system (Table 3); brake re-
663
action times for these drivers are presented in Fig. 5 and Table 5. Second,
664
brake response in terms of jerk and maximum deceleration could be
665
modeled based on pooled data of volunteer response, independent of
666
FCW system, but correlated to each other as presented in Fig. 13. One
667
only needs to select the percentile value of response relevant for the as-
668
sessment scheme. For pedestrian FCW safety beneﬁt assessments, data
669
from this study seems not only more relevant than data from car-to-
670
car collision experiments but also enables modeling of correlations
671
and dependencies that could not be modeled using separate studies
672
for each of the model parameters required.
673
However, the number of subjects per FCW system setting is limited
674
and further studies would be beneﬁcial. Further studies on pedestrian
675
FCW should also quantify the differences in brake response between
676
attentive and distracted drivers, the brake response in more complex
677
scenarios in comparison to the simple one used in this study, the inﬂu-
678
ence of the warning time on brake response, and the driver acceptance
679
of warnings.
